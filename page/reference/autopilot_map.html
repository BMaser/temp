Why we learn it: what is inside an autopilot

Reference page · plan row 1.6 · 10 parts of a multirotor autopilot, 4 parts of a fixed-wing autopilot · code from the PX4-Autopilot source code @ d28a642 [29] and the Project AirSim source code @ c80cff5 [30] (its documentation site: [41]), opened on GitHub on 2026-10-20

Goal. After this page you can name the parts of a drone's autopilot, say what question each part answers, find the part in PX4 [29] and in Project AirSim [30], and see which chapter of this course teaches the math it needs.

A The loop of a multirotor autopilot

An autopilot is a chain of parts that pass numbers to each other. Each part answers one question and hands its answer to the next part. Figure 1 draws the whole chain as a closed loop. The numbers 1 to 10 in the boxes are the row numbers of the table in part B.

Why four controllers in a row? Boxes 2 to 5 are four controllers. Each one is faster than the one before it. The PX4 User Guide [37], page Controller Diagrams [38], draws the same cascade for a multicopter: position, velocity, acceleration to attitude and thrust, attitude, rate, and then the mixer. The position controller (box 2) is the slowest: a drone needs time to move a meter. The rate controller (box 5) is the fastest: a drone starts to turn almost at once when the motors push differently. Each slower controller does not command the motors directly. It only says where the next, faster controller should aim: position goal → velocity goal → attitude goal → rate goal. A faster inner loop keeps the drone steady while the slower outer loop is still thinking. The estimator (box 10) sits outside, because all four controllers need to know where the drone is, how fast it moves, which way it points and how fast it turns.

What is on the drone and what is not. Boxes 1 to 7, 9 and 10 run on the drone, in PX4. Box 8 is the world: on a real drone it is the air and the frame; in a simulator it is a program that adds up the forces and moves the drone one time step. This is why a simulator can replace the world and leave the other nine boxes unchanged.

B The multirotor table: 10 parts

How to read this table. The table has ten rows, one for each box of Figure 1; the row number is the box number. Read a row from left to right. Part is the name. What it answers is the question that part solves. The math, in letters is the main formula of the part; the letters are explained below the table. PX4 and Project AirSim name a file and its line numbers; the link opens the file at those lines on GitHub, at the version named in the column title (a version is a GitHub commit; the lines are valid for that version only). Course says where this course teaches the math; a link opens the page, and a plain name means that the section is not written yet. Figure 2 draws every row of the table as one line.

The letters. A subscript sp means "set point", the goal. x, v, a are the position (m), the velocity (m/s) and the acceleration (m/s²) of the drone; xsp, vsp, asp are the goals for them. Kp, Kd and Kff are gains, fixed numbers that say how strongly a controller reacts to the error, to the change of the error, and to the goal itself (feed-forward). ev is the velocity error vsp − v (m/s). The sign ∫ means "the sum of the errors over time"; it removes a constant error. v̇ and ω̇ are the changes per second of v and of ω. T is the thrust (N); Tc is the thrust size the controller asks for; body_z is the unit arrow that points along the drone's own up axis. q is the attitude (a quaternion, four numbers); qd is the wanted attitude; qe = q⁻¹ ⊗ qd is the attitude error (⊗ is the product of two quaternions); imag(qe) is its three imaginary numbers; e = 2·imag(qe) is the turn that is still missing, as three angles in radians. ω is the turn rate (rad/s) and ωsp its goal; ∘ means "multiply each of the three numbers by its own gain". τ is the torque, the twisting force (N·m). u is the vector of the four motor commands; c is the wanted thrust and three torques (4 numbers); B is the effectiveness matrix (how much thrust and torque each motor makes); B⁺ is its pseudo-inverse; utrim and ctrim are the values for hover. CT is the thrust coefficient (no unit), ρ the air density (kg/m³), n the rotor speed (rev/s), d the rotor diameter (m). F is a force (N), m the mass (kg), Δt the length of one time step (s), and ← means "is replaced by". R is the rotation matrix from the body to the world, Δv is the velocity change that the IMU measured in one step (m/s), and g is the gravity (m/s²). f is the specific force that an accelerometer measures (m/s²).

C The fixed-wing table: 4 parts (kept apart)

A fixed-wing aircraft has no rotors that tilt its thrust. It turns by moving control surfaces (aileron, elevator, rudder), and its lift depends on the speed of the air over the wings. So its autopilot has other parts. The multirotor and the fixed-wing parts are never mixed in this course. Figure 3 draws the fixed-wing loop.

How to read this table. The table has four rows, F1 to F4, one for each part drawn in Figure 3. The columns are the same as in the multirotor table: the part, what it answers, the main formula, the PX4 file with its lines [29], the Project AirSim file (in this table a whole file, without lines) [30], and where the course teaches it. V is the airspeed (m/s), V̇ its change per second, g the gravity (m/s²), ḣ the climb rate (m/s). φ is the bank angle (rad), R the radius of the turn (m). ρ is the air density (kg/m³), S the wing area (m²), CL the lift coefficient (no unit), L the lift (N). Figure 3 draws every row.

D How each section page starts from now on

The "Why you learn this" box. Every section page of the course opens, right after its goal, with a box called "Why you learn this". The box has a table of three columns: the autopilot part (a row of this map, as a link), what this page gives to that part, and where the part is in PX4 [29] and in Project AirSim [30]. A small figure draws the loop of Figure 1 with this page's rows highlighted. The box ends with one sentence that starts "Without this page, …" and says what would be missing. The links of the box look like autopilot_map.html#row-3 (multirotor) or autopilot_map.html#row-F4 (fixed wing); the link opens this page at that row of the table. Use the box to know why you are reading the page before you read it.
