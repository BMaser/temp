2.2.13 Jerk: how fast the acceleration changes

On page 2.2a the acceleration was the rate of change of the velocity, a = dv/dt. The same step can be taken once more. The jerk is the rate of change of the acceleration: j = da/dt. Its unit is m/s³, read "meters per second per second per second". A positive jerk means that the acceleration is growing. A negative jerk means that it is shrinking. Over a time step Δt the average jerk is javg = Δa / Δt, in the same way as the average acceleration is Δv / Δt.

The slope and area rules of 2.2.4 now have one more step. The slope of x(t) is v, the slope of v(t) is a, and the slope of a(t) is j. In the other direction, the area under j(t) is Δa, the area under a(t) is Δv, and the area under v(t) is Δx. The planner of PX4 [29] sends all four numbers for each of the three directions. The file describes the message in these words: Trajectory setpoint in NED frame. Input to PID position controller. Needs to be kinematically consistent and feasible for smooth flight. [50, line 7]. The field for the jerk has the description in meters/second^3 (for logging only) [50, line 19].

How to read this table. The table has four rows, one for each of the four numbers of a trajectory. Read a row from left to right: the name of the quantity and its letter, its unit, the rule that gives it from the row above, and the name of the field in PX4's trajectory setpoint [50]. It matters for flying because the planner of 2.2.14 limits the last two rows: the acceleration (to A) and the jerk (to J). Here is every symbol. x is the position in meters (m). v is the velocity in m/s. a is the acceleration in m/s². j is the jerk in m/s³. dx/dt, dv/dt and da/dt are the rates of change (the slopes) of x, v and a. Constant jerk. If the jerk is constant, the area rules give three equations, in the same way as the four equations of page 2.2a came from a constant acceleration. a = a0 + j·t, v = v0 + a0·t + ½·j·t² and x = x0 + v0·t + ½·a0·t² + (1/6)·j·t³. The letters a0, v0 and x0 are the acceleration, the velocity and the position at the start of the part, and t is the time since that start. The PX4 planner uses exactly these three lines to move along each part of its curve (jt is the jerk with its sign, t2 is t², t3 is t³) [29, VelocitySmoothing.cpp:233-235]:

traj.a = a0 + jt * t;
traj.v = v0 + a0 * t + 0.5f * jt * t2;
traj.x = x0 + v0 * t + 0.5f * a0 * t2 + 1.f / 6.f * jt * t3;

Why a controller limits the jerk. Suppose that a planner asks for a jump of the acceleration, from 0 to 3.0 m/s² in no time. A jump has an infinite jerk. PX4 gives its aim in these words: Vehicle flight characteristics are better if the corresponding desired setpoint changes as a "ramp". [48, line 25]. It adds that a badly tuned setpoint may result in either very jerky or very unresponsive reactions to setpoint changes [48, line 31].

The reason below is the explanation of this course. It is not a sentence of the PX4 pages. A multicopter makes a horizontal acceleration by tilting. PX4 turns the acceleration that the velocity controller asks for into a thrust setpoint and an attitude setpoint: The acceleration setpoints generated by the velocity controller will be converted to thrust and attitude setpoints. [38, line 53]. The drone has to rotate to the new tilt, and every motor has to change its speed. PX4's simulator and Project AirSim both contain a first-order lag between the command of a motor and the motor itself. Here is the lag in PX4's simulator [29, sih.cpp:383]:

_u[i] = _u[i] + dt / _T_TAU * (u_sp - _u[i]); // first order transfer function with time constant tau

Here is the lag in Project AirSim [30, first_order_filter.hpp:72]:

output_ = static_cast<T>((output_ * alpha) + (input_ * (1.0 - alpha)));

In both lines the new output moves only a part of the way toward the new input. So a jump in the command reaches the motor only after a delay, and a ramp is followed more closely than a jump. Figure 2 compares a jump with a ramp that both bring the drone to 3.0 m/s.

2.2.14 The S-curve: three parts, three numbers

The jerk-limited trajectory of PX4 generates symmetric smooth S-curves where the jerk and acceleration limits are always guaranteed [47, line 4]. The same page says that this trajectory type is always enabled in autonomous modes like Mission mode [47, line 6]. The curve has this name because the graph of the velocity looks like the letter S.

Three limits describe the curve. A is the largest acceleration, in m/s². J is the largest jerk, in m/s³. V is the speed to reach, in m/s. The source code of PX4 splits the motion into three parts and gives their durations in seconds: T1 is the Increasing acceleration, T2 is the Constant acceleration and T3 is the Decreasing acceleration [29, VelocitySmoothing.hpp:208-210].

Take a drone at rest with a = 0 that must reach the speed V. Part 1 uses the jerk +J, so a grows from 0 to A. Part 2 uses the jerk 0, so a stays at A. Part 3 uses the jerk −J, so a falls from A back to 0. The area under a(t) is the speed gained. Part 1 is a triangle with the area ½·A·T1, and part 3 is the same triangle turned around. Part 2 is a rectangle with the area A·T2. The three areas must add up to V.

Part 1 takes T1 = A / J, because a grows by J every second until it reaches A. The triangle has the area ½ × A × (A / J) = A² / (2·J). The two triangles together give A² / J. So V = A² / J + A·T2, and the three durations are:

T1 = T3 = A / J    T2 = V / A − A / J    T = T1 + T2 + T3 = V / A + A / J

These formulas hold when the acceleration A is really reached. That is the case if V ≥ A² / J. If V is smaller, the acceleration turns around before it reaches A. Then there is no part 2, and T1 = T3 = √(V / J), T2 = 0, T = 2·√(V / J) and the largest acceleration is √(J·V). PX4 contains both cases in a few lines. Here is the line that cuts the first part so that the acceleration does not pass A (a0 is the acceleration at the start, which is 0 in our case) [29, VelocitySmoothing.cpp:61-65]:

float accel_T1 = a0 + j_max * T1;
float T1_new = T1;

if (accel_T1 > a_max) {
	T1_new = (a_max - a0) / j_max;

With a0 = 0 the line T1_new = (a_max - a0) / j_max is our T1 = A / J. Here is the line that gives the length of part 2 (v3 is the speed to gain, den is a0 + j_max * T1, which is A in our case) [29, VelocitySmoothing.cpp:142-145]:

float den = a0 + j_max * T1;

if (math::abs_t(den) > FLT_EPSILON) {
	T2 = (-0.5f * T1 * T1 * j_max - T1 * T3 * j_max - T1 * a0 + 0.5f * T3 * T3 * j_max - T3 * a0 + v3) / den;

Put a0 = 0 and T3 = T1 = A / J into this line. The three terms with T1 and T3 add up to −A² / J, so the line says T2 = (V − A² / J) / A = V / A − A / J. This agrees with the formula above.

Worked example, with the default limits of PX4 (A = 3.0 m/s², J = 4.0 m/s³, rows 3 and 4 of 2.2.15) and V = 6.0 m/s. The speed V = 6.0 m/s is a value that the user may choose; it is not the default (the default cruise speed is 5.0 m/s). We use 6.0 because every number of this example then comes out exact.

  1. Is A reached? A² / J = 3.0² / 4.0 = 9.0 / 4.0 = 2.25 m/s. The speed V = 6.0 m/s is larger, so part 2 exists.
  2. Part 1 and part 3: T1 = A / J = 3.0 / 4.0 = 0.75 s and T3 = 0.75 s.
  3. Part 2: T2 = V / A − A / J = 6.0 / 3.0 − 0.75 = 2.0 − 0.75 = 1.25 s.
  4. Total time: T = 0.75 + 1.25 + 0.75 = 2.75 s.
  5. Speed after part 1: v1 = ½·J·T1² = ½ × 4.0 × 0.75² = 2.0 × 0.5625 = 1.125 m/s. Speed after part 2: v2 = V − v1 = 6.0 − 1.125 = 4.875 m/s. The check by part 2 gives the same: v1 + A·T2 = 1.125 + 3.0 × 1.25 = 1.125 + 3.75 = 4.875 m/s.
  6. Distance of part 1 (equation x of 2.2.13 with a0 = 0): x1 = (1/6) × 4.0 × 0.75³ = 0.28125 m.
  7. Distance of part 2 (a constant, equation 2 of page 2.2a): x2 = v1·T2 + ½·A·T2² = 1.125 × 1.25 + ½ × 3.0 × 1.25² = 1.40625 + 2.34375 = 3.75 m.
  8. Distance of part 3 (start a0 = A, jerk −J): x3 = v2·T3 + ½·A·T3² − (1/6)·J·T3³ = 4.875 × 0.75 + ½ × 3.0 × 0.75² − 0.28125 = 3.65625 + 0.84375 − 0.28125 = 4.21875 m.
  9. Total distance: x = 0.28125 + 3.75 + 4.21875 = 8.25 m. The velocity of an S-curve is symmetric about its middle, so the average speed is V / 2. The check V·T / 2 = 6.0 × 2.75 / 2 = 8.25 m gives the same.

  2. At the border between the two cases, both formulas give the same T. Can you find that T?
  3. After you solve, which statement must you check to be sure that you used the right formula?

Show the solution
  1. Why this step: first find the border. At V = A² / J the acceleration A is just reached. A² / J = 3.0² / 4.0 = 2.25 m/s. Its duration is T = V / A + A / J = 2.25 / 3.0 + 0.75 = 1.5 s, and the other formula gives the same: 2·A / J = 2 × 3.0 / 4.0 = 1.5 s.
  2. Why this step: flight 1 lasted 2.75 s, which is more than 1.5 s, so A is reached. Solve T = V / A + A / J for V. V = (T − A / J) × A = (2.75 − 0.75) × 3.0 = 6.0 m/s.

  4. Why this step: the choice of the case was made with T, so check it with the result. In flight 2 the largest acceleration is J × T / 2 = 4.0 × 1.0 / 2 = 2.0 m/s², which is below A = 3.0, as the case needs.

2.2.15 The limits that PX4 publishes

PX4 keeps its limits in parameters. A parameter is a named number that the user may change. Every parameter has a default (the value used when nobody changes it), a unit, and a smallest and a largest value that it may have. The files that define the parameters print all of these. For the planner of automatic flight (missions, return to home, "go to") PX4 takes A, J and V from these parameters. The PX4 page lists the constraints of the XY-plane as MPC_JERK_AUTO for the jerk and MPC_ACC_HOR for the acceleration [47, lines 44-47]. The code sets them in these lines [29, FlightTaskAuto.cpp:829-831]:

_position_smoothing.setMaxAccelerationXY(_param_mpc_acc_hor.get()); // TODO : Should be computed using heading

The speed V comes from the cruise speed. The code makes sure that the cruise speed is not above the largest speed (_param_mpc_xy_vel_max is the parameter MPC_XY_VEL_MAX) [29, FlightTaskAuto.cpp:437-438]:

2.2.16 What the jerk limit costs: a straight run, step by step

The jerk limit makes the speed change smoother, and it makes it longer. Here is the first comparison. A drone flies a straight run of L = 100 m from rest to rest. It speeds up to V = 6.0 m/s, cruises, and slows down to rest again. The speeding up and the slowing down use the same limits. The first row of the table uses the constant acceleration A = 3.0 m/s² of page 2.2a. The second row uses the S-curve of 2.2.14 with the PX4 defaults A = 3.0 m/s² and J = 4.0 m/s³ [29].

2.2.17 How fast may the drone be, with a distance d to go?

The planner of PX4 does not only follow the cruise speed. Its speed limit for a leg depends on the distance d that is left to the target. The code says so in its own words: A valid position setpoint generates a velocity target using conservative motion constraints. [29, PositionSmoothing.cpp:194]. The function that computes the limit is computeMaxSpeedFromDistance. Here are its four lines (accel is A, jerk is J, braking_distance is d, and final_speed is the speed wanted at the target, 0 for a stop) [29, TrajMath.hpp:64-67]:

auto sqr = [](float f) {return f * f;};
float b =  4.0f * sqr(accel) / jerk;
float c = - 2.0f * accel * braking_distance - sqr(final_speed);
float max_speed = 0.5f * (-b + sqrtf(sqr(b) - 4.0f * c));

Read the lines as a formula, with the final speed 0. Let b = 4·A² / J. Then c = −2·A·d, and the largest speed is the positive root of v² + b·v + c = 0:

vmax = ½ · ( −b + √(b² + 8·A·d) )    with   b = 4·A² / J

The comment above the function says how it thinks about braking: We assume a constant acceleration profile with a delay of 2*accel/jerk [29, TrajMath.hpp:50]. In letters: braking with the constant deceleration A, plus a delay of 2·A / J seconds during which the drone still moves at the speed v. The distance is d = v² / (2·A) + (2·A / J)·v. Multiply by 2·A, and you get v² + (4·A² / J)·v = 2·A·d, which is the equation above.

Example with A = 3.0 m/s² and J = 4.0 m/s³. First b = 4 × 3.0² / 4.0 = 9.0. With d = 10.0 m to go: vmax = ½ × ( −9.0 + √(9.0² + 8 × 3.0 × 10.0) ) = ½ × ( −9.0 + √321.0 ) = ½ × ( −9.0 + 17.916 ) = 4.458 m/s. With d = 15.0 m to go: vmax = ½ × ( −9.0 + √(9.0² + 8 × 3.0 × 15.0) ) = ½ × ( −9.0 + √441.0 ) = ½ × ( −9.0 + 21.0 ) = 6.0 m/s. So the cruise speed 6.0 m/s is allowed from 15.0 m before the target. The S-curve of 2.2.14 itself needs only 8.25 m to stop from 6.0 m/s (the same distance as the speed-up). The formula keeps 15.0 m, almost twice as much; this is what the code calls conservative. Figure 9 draws the curve.

When the drone lags behind. A real drone may be behind the planned trajectory. The planner then slows its own clock. The factor is 1 − e / emax, where e is the distance between the drone and the planned position (m), and emax is the parameter MPC_XY_ERR_MAX (row 10; the factor is held between 0 and 1). The file says The integration speed of the trajectory setpoint is linearly reduced with the horizontal position tracking error. [29, lines 120-121]. The code line is [29, PositionSmoothing.cpp:321]:

time_stretch_xy = 1.f - math::constrain(position_error_xy / _max_allowed_horizontal_error, 0.f, 1.f);

THINK 14 · A GATE IN NED, AND THE TIME THE PATH FOLLOWER NEEDS

Situation. A program flies a drone in Project AirSim [30, 51]. In the course axes (east, north, up) the drone is at (10, 35, 3) m and the gate is at (40, 15, 8) m. The program calls move_to_position_async(north, east, down, velocity) with the speed 5.0 m/s. Use the rules of 2.2.19: the call becomes a path with one point, the follower sends a velocity vector of the length 5.0 m/s toward the point, and inside the braking distance it flies with braking_vel (defaults of the header). Figure 14 shows the drone and the gate from above, and the two times.

Questions. (a) Which numbers does the call need, relative to the drone? (b) What velocity vector does the follower send (in NED), and what is the distance? (c) How long would the flight take at 5.0 m/s all the way? (d) How long does it take with the braking rule?

Think first.

  1. The call wants the point in NED. Which of the three differences changes its sign, and which two change their names?
  2. The vector must point at the gate and have the length 5.0 m/s. What do you divide by?
  3. Is 5.0 m/s above braking_vel and above min_vel_for_braking?
HintUse N = north difference, E = east difference, D = −(up difference). Then the unit vector is (N, E, D) divided by the distance.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: convert each difference to NED (2.2.19). The north difference is N = 15 − 35 = −20, the east difference is E = 40 − 10 = 30, and the down difference is D = −(8 − 3) = −5. The gate is higher, so D is negative.
  2. Why this step: the distance is the length of the difference vector. √((−20)² + 30² + (−5)²) = √(400 + 900 + 25) = √1325 = 36.40 m.
  3. Why this step: the follower multiplies the unit vector by the speed. 5.0 × (−20, 30, −5) / 36.40 = (−2.75, 4.12, −0.69) m/s as (v_north, v_east, v_down).
  4. Why this step: with the same speed all the way, the time is the distance divided by the speed. 36.40 / 5.0 = 7.28 s.
  5. Why this step: the braking distance is the speed times 0.5, held between 1 and 3 m. clip(5.0 × 0.5, 1, 3) = 2.5 m. The speed 5.0 m/s is above braking_vel (1.0) and above min_vel_for_braking (3), so the last 2.5 m are flown at 1.0 m/s. (36.40 − 2.5) / 5.0 + 2.5 / 1.0 = 6.78 + 2.5 = 9.28 s.

Check your answer. The braking rule adds time: the last 2.5 m take 2.5 s instead of 0.5 s. 2.5 / 1.0 − 2.5 / 5.0 = 2.5 − 0.5 = 2.0 s, and 7.28 + 2.0 = 9.28 s. A wrong sign of D (+5 instead of −5) would send the drone 5 m down, away from the gate, which the gate height 8 m above the 3 m start makes easy to see.

Show the answer2.5 m/s is not above min_vel_for_braking (3), so the leg is not changed. The time is 36.40 / 2.5 = 14.56 s. The faster call, 5.0 m/s, arrives 5.28 s earlier even with its slow end.

★ Summary

  • Jerk. j = da/dt in m/s³. With a constant jerk: a = a₀ + j·t, v = v₀ + a₀·t + ½·j·t², x = x₀ + v₀·t + ½·a₀·t² + (1/6)·j·t³.
  • S-curve from rest. If V ≥ A² / J: T1 = T3 = A / J, T2 = V / A − A / J, T = V / A + A / J. Otherwise T1 = T3 = √(V / J) and T2 = 0. The distance is x = ½·(v₀ + V)·T.
  • Cost of the jerk limit. A / J more time for each speed change, compared with the constant acceleration A.
  • PX4 defaults in automatic flight [29]. V = 5.0 m/s (MPC_XY_CRUISE), A = 3.0 m/s² (MPC_ACC_HOR), J = 4.0 m/s³ (MPC_JERK_AUTO), largest speed 12.0 m/s (MPC_XY_VEL_MAX).
  • Speed limit near the target. vmax = ½·(−b + √(b² + 8·A·d)) with b = 4·A² / J.
  • Fixed wings. No jerk limit was found; the limits are airspeeds and climb and sink rates.
  • Project AirSim [30, 51]. NED and SI units; the state has pose, twist and accels; a move command has a velocity vector or a target with one speed.
