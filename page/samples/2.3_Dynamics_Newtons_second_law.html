2.3.1 Newton's second law as a vector sum

A force F is a push or a pull. It has a size and a direction, so we draw it as an arrow. The unit is the newton (N): one newton gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s². A force gets bigger when the push gets harder.

The mass m of the drone is how much it resists a change of its motion. The unit is the kilogram (kg). A payload makes m bigger. Mass does not change from place to place; weight does (2.3.3). The acceleration a is the rate of change of the velocity, in m/s² (2.2.3).

Several forces act on the drone at the same time. The net force ΣF ("sigma F") is their sum, added as arrows. Newton's second law says that the net force gives the drone an acceleration in the same direction, and that the acceleration is smaller when the mass is bigger:

a = ΣF / m

MIT 16.00 [24] states the law in its thrust section: "force (F) is equal to mass (m) times acceleration (a)". NASA Glenn [21] applies it to a ball in flight: the net vertical force is the weight plus the drag, and the acceleration follows from it. In the ISAE-SUPAERO course [70, C1 M2 "Newton's second law" video] the law is "the vectorial sum of those forces is equal to the mass m of the system multiplied by the acceleration" of its center of mass, observed in an inertial frame; the course adds that for flight mechanics the Earth is a very good inertial frame. We use the Earth the same way.

One axis at a time, then vectors. On a straight line, give each force a sign (the sign rule above), add the signed numbers, and divide by m. In space, do the same for each axis: aₓ = ΣFₓ / m, and so on. Or add the arrows and divide the resulting arrow by m (Try it 2).

What the simulators do. The most complete form in the code is the sum of three groups of forces, divided by the mass. PX4's simulator SIH [39] adds the weight as a force; Project AirSim adds gravity as an acceleration. Both give the same number.

PX4 and Project AirSim use this form because they treat the drone as one rigid body. Project AirSim's documentation [42] says that Fast Physics models "the drone as a cuboid body frame with attached rotors treated as point masses". One sum of forces is then enough: no blade, no wire and no battery is modeled separately.

Three cases. What the net force does to the velocity depends on the angle between ΣF and the velocity v. The ISAE-SUPAERO video shows the same facts with an apple: a series of stills a fixed time apart, with the same change of velocity Δv in every step.

TRY IT 1 · WARM-UP · THE SAME FORCE ON TWO MASSES

A bench test pushes a racing drone of mass m = 0.8 kg with a net force of 3.6 N. Find the acceleration. Then find it for the same drone with a camera and a battery that make the mass 1.6 kg, for the same net force.

Show solution
  1. a = ΣF / m = 3.6 / 0.8 = 4.50 m/s².
  2. Twice the mass, the same force: a = ΣF / m = 3.6 / 1.6 = 2.25 m/s². The acceleration halves when the mass doubles.
TRY IT 2 · MEDIUM · TWO HORIZONTAL FORCES AT RIGHT ANGLES

A drone of mass m = 2.0 kg hovers: thrust and weight balance. A crosswind pushes it with 3.0 N toward the east, and a tether pulls it with 4.0 N toward the north. Both forces are horizontal. Questions. (a) Find the size of the net horizontal force. (b) Find the size of the acceleration. (c) Find the direction of the acceleration, as an angle from east toward north. (d) Find the two components of the acceleration and check that they give the same size.

Show solution
  1. Why this step: the two forces are at a right angle, so they add like the sides of a right triangle. ΣF = √(3.0² + 4.0²) = √25.00 = 5.00 N.
  2. Why this step: the acceleration is the net force divided by the mass. a = ΣF / m = 5.00 / 2.0 = 2.50 m/s².
  3. Why this step: the acceleration points along the net force, so the angle comes from the two components of the force. θ = atan(Fy / Fx) = atan(4.0 / 3.0) = 53.1° from east toward north.
  4. Why this step: divide each component by the mass, then add the components as a right triangle. ax = 3.0 / 2.0 = 1.50 m/s² and ay = 4.0 / 2.0 = 2.00 m/s², and √(1.50² + 2.00²) = √6.25 = 2.50 m/s². It agrees with step 2.

Check your answer. The 3-4-5 triangle fits: 5.00 is longer than either side and shorter than their sum 7.0. The angle is above 45° because the north force is the bigger one.

TRY IT 3 · HARD · EQUAL STEPS OF VELOCITY, TWO DIRECTIONS OF FORCE

A drone of mass m = 1.2 kg flies level to the east at 10.0 m/s. A steady net force of 6.0 N acts on it. A camera takes a still every Δt = 0.50 s. Look at two cases. Case P: the force points north, at a right angle to the velocity. Case Q: the force points east, along the velocity. Questions. (a) Find the change of velocity Δv in each step. (b) Find the velocity after one and after two steps in case Q. (c) Find the speed and the direction after one and after two steps in case P. (d) Which case is a level turn, and which case changes the speed most?

Think first. Each still adds the same Δv. Does adding Δv at a right angle to v make the arrow longer by the full Δv?

Show solution
  1. Why this step: the force is steady, so the acceleration is the same in every step, and so is the change of velocity. a = ΣF / m = 6.0 / 1.2 = 5.00 m/s² and Δv = a·Δt = 5.00 × 0.50 = 2.50 m/s per step, in the direction of the force.
  2. Why this step: in case Q the arrows lie on one line, so the speeds just add. 10.0 + 2.50 = 12.5 m/s after one step and 12.5 + 2.50 = 15.0 m/s after two. The path stays straight.
  3. Why this step: in case P, Δv is at a right angle to v, so the new velocity is the hypotenuse of a right triangle. After one step: √(10.0² + 2.50²) = √106.25 = 10.31 m/s, turned by atan(2.50 / 10.0) = 14.0° toward the north. After two steps: √(10.0² + 5.00²) = √125.00 = 11.18 m/s, turned by atan(5.00 / 10.0) = 26.6°.
  4. Why this step: compare the speed gained in the two cases. After one step case Q gained 2.50 m/s and case P only 0.31 m/s, although both added the same Δv. Case P is the turn: the arrow rotates, and the speed changes only a little. A level turn at constant speed is the limit of very short steps, where the sideways Δv rotates the arrow and does not lengthen it. Case Q changes the speed most.

Check your answer. The speed in case P is the length of the hypotenuse, so it must be longer than 10.0 and shorter than 10.0 + 2.50 = 12.5: 10.31 is. And 11.18 is shorter than 10.0 + 5.00 = 15.0.

What if … the force in case P stays at a right angle to the velocity all the time, as it does in a real turn?

Show the answerThen the force turns with the velocity, and each step adds a Δv that is at a right angle to the new arrow. The speed stays constant and the path is a circle. The ISAE-SUPAERO apple thrown sideways is the same fact: gravity pulls down, the velocity arrow turns downward, and the speed hardly changes while the apple is near the top of its path [70, C1 M2 "Newton's second law" video].

2.3.2 The free-body diagram of a multirotor

To add forces you must first find them all. A free-body diagram does that. It shows one object alone, with one arrow for every force that acts on it. Draw it in five steps: (1) draw the drone as a small body and nothing else; (2) ask what pushes or pulls it, by touching it or from a distance; (3) draw an arrow from the body for each answer, with the length in proportion to the size; (4) choose a plus direction (here up); (5) write the sum, ΣF, with a sign for each arrow.

A multirotor in flight has three forces. Thrust T is the push of the air on the rotors, in N, when the rotors push air down; the four rotors together make it. It points along the vertical axis of the body, up when the drone is level, and it gets bigger when the rotors spin faster. Weight W is the pull of the Earth, in N. It points down, and it gets bigger when the mass is bigger (2.3.3). Drag D is the push of the air against the motion, in N. It points opposite to the velocity, and it gets bigger when the speed is bigger (2.3.6). Lift is the force of a wing; a multirotor has none worth drawing here, and wings are on a later page (plan row fly.7).

The simulators have the same three groups. PX4's SIH [39] sums _Fa_E + _q_E.rotateVector(_T_B) + weight_E: the aerodynamic force, the thrust rotated from the body frame into the world frame, and the weight (sih.cpp:686 @ d28a642 [29]). Project AirSim sums external_wrench + drag_wrench + lift_drag_wrench and adds gravity as an acceleration (fast_physics.cpp:521-525 @ c80cff5 [30]). In the code the thrust is the vector (0, 0, −_T_MAX·Σu): no push along x and y, and a minus sign along z. PX4 numbers its body axes front, right, down, so minus z is up (2.2c).

How to read this table. The table has three rows, one for each arrow of the diagram. Read a row from left to right: the force, its symbol and unit, where it points, what makes it bigger, and where the simulator code holds it. Here is every symbol. T is the total thrust of all rotors, W = m·g is the weight, and D is the drag, all in N. v is the velocity of the drone relative to the air, in m/s. m is the mass in kg and g the acceleration of gravity in m/s². Why the diagram helps. A drone is a complicated object, but for its motion as a whole only the sum matters. The diagram forces you to count each force once, and it shows the direction of each, which is where most sign mistakes start. Watch out. Draw only forces that act on the drone. The force that the drone exerts on the air is real, but it acts on the air; it belongs in the diagram of the air (2.3.7). And never draw an "acceleration force": the acceleration is the result of the sum, not another arrow.

Source for the three forces of a free-body diagram: MIT 16.00 [24] names weight, thrust and drag as the forces of an aircraft; NASA Glenn [21] shows the diagram of a ball with weight and drag, where "the only forces acting on the ball are the forces of weight and drag". The owner's notes [69], PDF page 32, draw the quadcopter diagram of Figure 2.3 with the weight M×g and the four rotor forces.

TRY IT 4 · MEDIUM · THE SAME SPEED UP AND DOWN

A drone of mass m = 1.2 kg flies at a steady 3 m/s, first straight up and then straight down. At this speed the drag is D = 2.0 N. Questions. (a) Find the weight. (b) Draw the diagram for each flight and find the thrust that the rotors must make going up. (c) Find the thrust going down. (d) Explain why the two thrusts differ although the speed is the same.

Show solution
  1. Why this step: the weight is needed in both flights. W = m·g = 1.2 × 9.8062 = 11.767 N.
  2. Why this step: a steady speed means no acceleration, so the sum is zero: a = ΣF / m = 0 / 1.2 = 0.00 m/s². Going up, the drag acts down, so the sum is ΣF = T − W − D = 0. Solve for T: T = W + D = 11.767 + 2.0 = 13.767 N.
  3. Why this step: going down, the drone moves against the other direction, so the drag points up, against the motion. Now ΣF = T − W + D = 0, so T = W − D = 11.767 − 2.0 = 9.767 N.
  4. Why this step: the drag always opposes the velocity, so it changes sides when the velocity does. Going up the rotors must carry the weight and beat the drag; going down the drag helps to carry the weight. The difference is 13.767 − 9.767 = 4.000 N, twice the drag.

Check your answer. In each panel the arrows up add to the arrows down: 13.767 = 11.767 + 2.0 and 9.767 + 2.0 = 11.767.

What if … the drag were 3.0 N?

Show the answerGoing up, T = 11.767 + 3.0 = 14.767 N; going down, T = 11.767 − 3.0 = 8.767 N. The two thrusts now differ by 6.0 N, again twice the drag.
TRY IT 5 · HARD · THE ACCELERATION CHANGES AS THE SPEED FALLS

A drone of mass m = 1.2 kg flies straight up at 4.0 m/s when its thrust drops to a steady 10.4 N (less than its weight). The drag is D = k·v² with k = 0.05 N·s²/m². Questions. (a) Find the drag at 4.0 m/s. (b) Find the acceleration at that moment. (c) Is the drone speeding up or slowing down, and which way does it move? (d) Find the acceleration when the drone reaches v = 0. (e) Give two times between which the drone stops, if the acceleration were constant at the first value and at the second value.

Think first. Which way does the drag point while the drone still moves up? What happens to the drag when v falls? Can one constant acceleration describe the whole stop?

Show solution
  1. Why this step: the drag uses the speed now. D = k·v² = 0.05 × 4.0² = 0.80 N, pointing down because the drone moves up.
  2. Why this step: list all three forces with up as plus: thrust up, weight down, drag down. W = m·g = 1.2 × 9.8062 = 11.767 N. ΣF = T − W − D = 10.4 − 11.767 − 0.80 = −2.167 N, so a = ΣF / m = −2.167 / 1.2 = −1.81 m/s².
  3. Why this step: compare the sign of a with the sign of v. The drone still moves up (v is plus) but a is minus, so the velocity shrinks: the drone is slowing down while it still climbs.
  4. Why this step: at v = 0 the drag is zero, and the sum has only thrust and weight. ΣF = 10.4 − 11.767 = −1.367 N, so a = −1.367 / 1.2 = −1.14 m/s². The acceleration is smaller in size than at the start, because the drag that helped to brake has disappeared.
  5. Why this step: the real acceleration lies between the two values, so the stopping time lies between two estimates. With the first value: t = v / |a| = 4.0 / 1.81 = 2.21 s. With the second: t = 4.0 / 1.14 = 3.51 s. The drone stops somewhere between 2.21 s and 3.51 s.

Check your answer. The drone must slow down, since the thrust is below the weight even without drag, so a is minus at every moment. And |a| must be largest when the drag is largest, at the start: 1.81 is more than 1.14.

What if … the thrust were exactly 11.767 N?

Show the answerAt v = 4.0 m/s the drag still brakes: ΣF = 11.767 − 11.767 − 0.80 = −0.80 N, so a = −0.80 / 1.2 = −0.67 m/s². The drone slows down until the drag has gone; with T = W it then stays at rest. Thrust equal to the weight does not mean "keep the speed" while the drag is on.

2.3.3 Weight: W = m·g

Weight W is the force with which the Earth pulls the drone. The unit is the newton. Mass m is how much matter the drone has; the unit is the kilogram. The two are not the same: a mass does not change from place to place, but its weight does. A scale in a shop shows kilograms, but it really measures a force and converts it with a fixed value of g (Try it 7).

The link between them is the acceleration of gravity g, in m/s². It is the acceleration of a body that falls with no other force on it. It gets bigger toward the poles and smaller with height. Page 2.1a, section 2.1.2 gives the versions of g; the most precise one is the WGS84 formula [45] of latitude φ and height h, and the course uses it in every exercise:

W = m·g(φ, h)

Two places are used on this page: the home field (φ = 45° N, h = 0 m) with g = 9.8062 m/s², and Denver (φ = 39.74° N, h = 1 600 m) with g = 9.7965 m/s². A rough value of 9.8 m/s² is enough to check an answer, but not to compute one: the third digit already differs.

What the simulators do. Project AirSim computes the gravity vector from the altitude only:

env_info.gravity =
    Vector3(0, 0, EarthUtils::GetGravity(env_info.geo_point.altitude));

environment.hpp:44-45 @ c80cff5 [30]. The vector has x = y = 0 and a plus z: Project AirSim's world frame has z down, so gravity points along +z.

PX4's SIH uses only the latitude, through the Somigliana formula (src/lib/lat_lon_alt/lat_lon_alt.hpp:119-126 @ d28a642 [29]); the weight enters the sum of forces as weight_E (sih.cpp:641-646). They use these simpler forms because one input is enough for a simulated flight near one place: AirSim has the height and PX4 SIH has the latitude. The full formula of this course takes both.

2.3.4 Thrust from the rotors and the thrust-to-weight ratio

Each rotor pushes air down and gets a push up. The thrust of one rotor Fi (i = 1 to 4) is that push in N. The thrust of the drone is the sum T = F1 + F2 + F3 + F4; the owner's notes [69] write the same sum on PDF page 33. The sum is the number that enters the free-body diagram of 2.3.2. Which rotor makes how much matters for turning (chapter 3), not for the sum.

What decides the thrust of a rotor? The most complete formula in the simulators is the one of Project AirSim. It gives the largest thrust of a rotor from four numbers: the thrust coefficient CT (no unit; how well the propeller turns speed into push), the air density ρ (kg/m³), the revolutions per second n (1/s) and the propeller diameter D (m):

F = CT·ρ·n²·D⁴

float revs_per_second = max_rpm / 60.0f;
max_speed_square = (float)std::pow(revs_per_second * 2 * M_PI, 2);
max_thrust =
    (float)(coeff_of_thrust * kBaseAirDensity * revs_per_second *
            revs_per_second * std::pow(propeller_diameter, 4));

rotor.hpp:85-89 @ c80cff5 [30]. The code uses the density at sea level, kBaseAirDensity, for the largest thrust. The same law holds at any speed n (2.1.8).

Three things follow. The thrust grows with the square of the rotor speed: twice the speed gives four times the thrust. It grows with the air density, so it falls with height (Think 1). And it grows with the fourth power of the diameter. The owner's notes agree on the square of the speed (F = kF·ω², PDF page 33 [69]); there ω is the rotor speed, which the notes give in rpm (PDF page 32), and kF collects CT, ρ and D. The law has the same shape in any unit of speed; only the number kF changes.

PX4's SIH uses a simpler law for a quadrotor. Each motor has a command ui between 0 and 1, and the thrust is linear in the command:

_T_B = Vector3f(0.0f, 0.0f, -_T_MAX * (+_u[0] + _u[1] + _u[2] + _u[3]));
_Mt_B = Vector3f(_L_ROLL * _T_MAX * (-_u[0] + _u[1] + _u[2] - _u[3]),
		 _L_PITCH * _T_MAX * (+_u[0] - _u[1] + _u[2] - _u[3]),
		 _Q_MAX * (+_u[0] + _u[1] - _u[2] - _u[3]));

sih.cpp:459-462 @ d28a642 [29]. _T_MAX is the largest thrust of one rotor. The first line is the thrust: T = TMAX·(u0 + u1 + u2 + u3). The second line holds the torques (chapter 3).

PX4 uses this simpler form because the SIH plant has its own fixed law from command to thrust; the controller in PX4 can linearise a real motor with a parameter (THR_MDL_FAC, page 2.1b), but the SIH quadrotor needs none. For a real or a Gazebo motor the quadratic law is the better model.

The thrust-to-weight ratio TWR (no unit) is the largest total thrust divided by the weight: TWR = Tmax / W. The owner's notes define it the same way (PDF page 31 and page 56 [69]). Why does it matter for the sum of forces? The drone can only make a net force up of Tmax − W. So its best vertical acceleration is

amax = (Tmax − W) / m = (TWR·W − W) / m = (TWR − 1)·g

because W = m·g. With TWR = 1 the drone can only hover; with TWR = 2 it can accelerate upward at 1 g. A second use: if thrust is linear in the command, the hover command is uh = 1 / TWR; if it is quadratic in the speed, the hover speed is √(1 / TWR) of the largest speed. PX4's default hover thrust, MPC_THR_HOVER, is 0.5 (multicopter_position_control_params.yaml:5-19 @ d28a642 [29]; the controller diagrams [38] describe it as the vertical thrust needed to hover); that is the value of TWR = 2 with a linear law.

2.3.5 One axis: hover, climb, descent and a changing mass

Take the vertical axis with up as plus. Two forces act when drag is small: the thrust T up and the weight W down. Newton's law gives the vertical acceleration a (plus is up):

a = (T − W) / m

and solved for the thrust that a wanted acceleration needs:

T = m·(g + a)

This is the rule of row 3 of the autopilot map: the velocity controller turns a wanted acceleration into a thrust. Read it in words: to hover (a = 0) the thrust equals the weight; to climb the thrust must also supply m·a; to descend slowly it may be smaller than the weight. The PX4 documentation [38] states the step in one sentence (quoted in the table of the start of this page).

The ISAE-SUPAERO course [70, C1 M3 "Load factor" video] shows a neat way to read the same equation. It rewrites Newton's law so that the forces other than weight equal "a pseudo weight" m·g·n, where the load factor n has no unit and measures how many times the weight those forces must be. For a multirotor on a vertical line the only force besides the weight is the thrust, so n = T / W = 1 + a / g. It is 1 in hover; above 1 in a climb (you feel heavier); below 1 in a descent (you feel lighter). The thrust-to-weight ratio of 2.3.4 is the largest n the drone can make.

2.3.6 Drag as a force against the velocity

Drag D is the force of the air against the motion, in N. Two facts are enough for the sum of forces. First, it points opposite to the velocity relative to the air (not relative to the ground): a drone that hovers in a wind of 5 m/s feels drag although its ground speed is zero. Second, it gets bigger with the speed v (m/s). There are two common forms. The linear form, D = k1·v, has the constant k1 in N·s/m. The quadratic form, D = k2·v², has the constant k2 in N·s²/m². In the quadratic form, twice the speed gives four times the drag.

The full drag model is on page 2.1 (2.1.5 and 2.1.11) and is not repeated here. NASA Glenn [21] gives the drag equation D = CD·ρ·A·V²/2 for a ball, so the constant of the quadratic form collects k2 = ½·ρ·S·CD: air density ρ, reference area S and drag coefficient CD. On this page the constants are given numbers.

What the simulators do. Project AirSim computes the drag on each face of the body. The comment in the code says: "Drag vector magnitude is proportional to v^2, with direction opposite of velocity." (fast_physics.cpp:725 @ c80cff5 [30]). PX4's SIH uses the linear form, with the velocity relative to the air:

_Fa_E = -_KDV * _R_N2E * _v_apparent_N; // first order drag to slow down the aircraft
_Ma_B = -_KDW * _w_B; // first order angular damper

sih.cpp:464-465 @ d28a642 [29]. _KDV plays the role of k1 and _v_apparent_N is the velocity relative to the air. The second line damps the rotation (chapter 3). PX4 uses the linear form because the code calls it "first order drag to slow down the aircraft": a simple term that keeps the simulated drone from flying on forever, not a model of a body shape.

The two forms differ at high speed. Take k1 = 0.30 N·s/m and k2 = 0.06 N·s²/m². At 5.0 m/s both give the same drag: k1·v = 0.30 × 5.0 = 1.50 N and k2·v² = 0.06 × 5.0² = 1.50 N. At 10.0 m/s they part: 0.30 × 10.0 = 3.00 N against 0.06 × 10.0² = 6.00 N. At 20.0 m/s the gap is large: 0.30 × 20.0 = 6.00 N against 0.06 × 20.0² = 24.0 N. Figure 18 draws both curves.

Sign. Draw the drag arrow against the velocity. In a formula with up as plus, the force on the drone is −k2·v·|v|: its sign is the opposite of the sign of v. Going up (v plus) it is minus, so it pulls down; going down (v minus) it is plus.

Watch out. Use the speed relative to the air. With a headwind of 3.0 m/s and a ground speed of 5.0 m/s, the air speed is 8.0 m/s, and the drag follows the 8.0 (Try it 15).

2.3.7 Action and reaction: why a rotor makes thrust

Newton's third law says that forces come in pairs. If body A pushes on body B with a force, then B pushes back on A with a force of the same size and the opposite direction. The two forces act on different bodies. That is the key point: you cannot put both forces of a pair into one free-body diagram.

A rotor spins and pushes air down. The air pushes the rotor up with a force of the same size: that is the thrust T. MIT 16.00 [24] says it in one sentence: "Thrust (T) is produced therefore by accelerating a mass of air." The rotors act on the air, the air acts on the rotors, and only the second force is in the diagram of the drone.

How large is the push? Apply the second law to the air. In one second the rotors accelerate a mass of air Q (kg/s) from rest to a speed Δv (m/s) downward. The force on the air is mass times acceleration, and that is the change of speed per second times the mass: F = Q·Δv. By the third law, the thrust is the same size:

T = Q·Δv

This is a simple model. It counts the air that the rotors accelerate and the speed that the air gets, and it does not say how the spinning blades do it (that is the theory of the rotor disk, in the propulsion pages). The textbook of Leishman [1] covers it (not checked: the chapter pages could not be fetched).

The weight has a partner too. The Earth pulls the drone down with W. The partner of that force is the pull of the drone on the Earth, also W, upward, on the Earth. So thrust and weight are not a pair. They act on the same body. In hover they are equal because the net force is zero (second law), not because of the third law. In a climb they are unequal, while each force still has its equal partner on another body.

TRY IT 16 · MEDIUM · HOW MUCH AIR MUST THE ROTORS MOVE?

A drone of mass 1.2 kg hovers at the home field. Its rotors push air downward. Questions. (a) Find the thrust. (b) The air leaves the rotors at Δv = 8.0 m/s faster than it came. Find the mass of air per second, and the volume per second at the density 1.225 kg/m³. (c) Larger propellers push air at only 4.0 m/s. Find the mass of air per second then. (d) What do you learn about large slow propellers?

Show solution
  1. Why this step: in hover the thrust carries the weight. W = m·g = 1.2 × 9.8062 = 11.767 N, so T = 11.767 N.
  2. Why this step: solve T = Q·Δv for Q. Q = T / Δv = 11.767 / 8.0 = 1.471 kg/s. The volume is the mass divided by the density: 1.471 / 1.225 = 1.20 m³/s.
  3. Why this step: the same thrust with half the speed change. Q = 11.767 / 4.0 = 2.942 kg/s.
  4. Why this step: compare. Half the speed change needs twice the mass of air: 2.942 / 1.471 = 2.0. A large propeller reaches more air, so it can make the same thrust with a slower stream. The model says only this; what it means for power is left to the propulsion pages.

Check your answer. The units: kg/s times m/s is kg·m/s², which is N. So Q·Δv has the unit of a force.

What if … the drone climbed at a = 3.00 m/s² with the same Δv?

Show the answerThe thrust would be T = 15.367 N (2.3.5), so Q = 15.367 / 8.0 = 1.921 kg/s: 31 % more air, the same factor as the thrust.
TRY IT 17 · HARD · A DRONE FLYING INSIDE A SEALED BOX

A sealed box of mass M = 2.0 kg stands on a scale at the home field. Inside it, a drone of mass m = 1.2 kg flies. Treat the air in the box as massless. Questions. (a) What does the scale read while the drone hovers? (b) The drone climbs inside the box with a = +3.00 m/s². What does the scale read? (c) The drone descends with a = −2.00 m/s². What does it read? (d) Where does the push on the scale come from, in terms of action and reaction?

Think first. Draw the diagram of the whole system, box and drone together. Which forces from outside act on it? Does the sealed box know about the drone's rotors?

Show solution
  1. Why this step: the rotors push air down, the air pushes the floor of the box, and so the scale feels it. Looking at the whole system, only two outside forces act: the scale force N up and the weight of box and drone down. The box does not accelerate, and the drone has acceleration a, so the second law for the system is N − (M + m)·g = m·a.
  2. Why this step: hover has a = 0. (M + m)·g = 3.2 × 9.8062 = 31.380 N, so N = 31.380 N. The scale reads the full weight: a hovering drone adds its whole weight to the box.
  3. Why this step: in the climb a is plus. N = (M + m)·g + m·a = 31.380 + 1.2 × 3.00 = 31.380 + 3.600 = 34.980 N.
  4. Why this step: in the descent a is minus. N = 31.380 + 1.2 × (−2.00) = 31.380 − 2.400 = 28.980 N.
  5. Why this step: action and reaction. The rotors push air down with the thrust, and the air pushes the rotors up. The air that is pushed down hits the floor and pushes the floor down; the floor pushes the box, and the box pushes the scale. Nothing leaves the box, so every force the drone makes is passed on to the scale in the end. In the climb the drone makes more thrust than its weight, and the extra 3.600 N shows on the scale.

Check your answer. The climb reading minus the hover reading is 34.980 − 31.380 = 3.600 N, which equals m·a = 1.2 × 3.00 = 3.600 N.

What if … the drone were released and fell freely inside the box?

Show the answerWith a = −g for the drone, N = (M + m)·g + m·(−g) = M·g = 2.0 × 9.8062 = 19.612 N: the scale feels only the box, because a freely falling drone pushes on nothing.

2.3.8 Mixed problems

These five problems join the ideas of 2.3.1 to 2.3.7: payload, wind from the side, a fall from a height, a fast climb and a changing hover command. Try each on paper before you open the solution. Altitude enters in Think 1.

PROBLEM 1 · A PAYLOAD AND THE BEST CLIMB

The drone of Try it 9 (1.2 kg, Tmax = 30.0 N) carries a camera of 0.30 kg at the home field. Questions. (a) Find the weight. (b) Find the TWR; does it meet the rule of 2? (c) Find the best vertical acceleration. (d) Find the thrust for a climb of 6.00 m/s² and its share of Tmax.

Show solution
  1. Why this step: the camera adds to the mass first. m = 1.2 + 0.30 = 1.5 kg, then W = m·g = 1.5 × 9.8062 = 14.709 N.
  2. Why this step: the TWR compares the largest thrust with this weight. TWR = Tmax / W = 30.0 / 14.709 = 2.04. It is above 2, so the rule is met, just: the limit mass is 1.53 kg (Try it 10) and 1.53 − 1.2 = 0.33 kg is the largest payload.
  3. Why this step: the best acceleration uses all the thrust. amax = (Tmax − W) / m = (30.0 − 14.709) / 1.5 = 15.291 / 1.5 = 10.19 m/s².
  4. Why this step: the thrust for a wanted acceleration is T = m·(g + a). T = 1.5 × (9.8062 + 6.00) = 1.5 × 15.8062 = 23.709 N; its share is 23.709 / 30.0 = 0.79.

Check your answer. 23.709 N is more than the weight 14.709 N (the drone climbs) and less than 30.0 N (the motors can do it).

PROBLEM 2 · HOLDING POSITION IN A CROSSWIND

A drone of mass 1.2 kg must hold its place at the home field in a wind that pushes it with a horizontal force of Hw = 2.4 N toward the east. It tilts so that the thrust has a horizontal part that cancels this force. Questions. (a) Find the vertical and the horizontal part of the thrust. (b) Find the thrust and the tilt from the vertical. (c) Compare the thrust with the hover thrust. (d) Repeat for a wind force of 4.8 N.

Show solution
  1. Why this step: holding the place means ΣF = 0 on each axis. Vertically, the vertical part of the thrust carries the weight: V = W = m·g = 1.2 × 9.8062 = 11.767 N. Horizontally, the horizontal part cancels the wind: H = Hw = 2.4 N, toward the west.
  2. Why this step: the two parts are the sides of a right triangle, and the thrust is the hypotenuse. T = √(V² + H²) = √(11.767² + 2.4²) = √(138.462 + 5.760) = √144.222 = 12.009 N. The tilt from the vertical follows from the sides: θ = atan(H / V) = atan(2.4 / 11.767) = 11.5°.
  3. Why this step: compare with the hover thrust, which equals the weight. T / W = 12.009 / 11.767 = 1.021: the tilt costs 2.1 % more thrust.
  4. Why this step: the same steps with the stronger wind. T = √(11.767² + 4.8²) = √(138.462 + 23.040) = √161.502 = 12.708 N and θ = atan(4.8 / 11.767) = 22.2°. Doubling the wind force doubles the tangent of the tilt, while the thrust rises only to T / W = 12.708 / 11.767 = 1.080 of the hover thrust.

Watch out. The horizontal part is T·sin θ and the vertical part is T·cos θ when θ is measured from the vertical. The owner's notes [69] (PDF page 36) write F cos θ for the horizontal part and F sin θ for the vertical part, where θ is the tilt of the drone. With the tilt, the two are swapped: at θ = 0 (hover) the vertical part must be the whole force. The notes' form fits only an angle measured from the horizontal. How the tilt is chosen is on page 2.5.

PROBLEM 3 · THE MOTORS FAIL AT 20 m IN DENVER

A drone of mass 1.2 kg loses all thrust at a height of 20.0 m in Denver (g = 9.7965 m/s²) and falls straight down. The drag is quadratic, k2 = 0.06 N·s²/m². Questions. (a) Find the weight. (b) Ignore the drag: find the fall time and the final speed. (c) With the drag, find the acceleration at v = 10.0 m/s downward. (d) Find the speed at which the acceleration is zero, and say whether the result of (b) can be right.

Show solution
  1. Why this step: the weight is the only force when the drag is ignored. W = m·g = 1.2 × 9.7965 = 11.756 N.
  2. Why this step: without drag the acceleration is g and the equations of 2.2.9 apply. t = √(2·h / g) = √(2 × 20.0 / 9.7965) = √4.08 = 2.02 s and v = g·t = 9.7965 × 2.02 = 19.79 m/s.
  3. Why this step: with the drag, at 10.0 m/s the drag points up. D = k2·v² = 0.06 × 10.0² = 6.00 N. With up as plus, ΣF = D − W = 6.00 − 11.756 = −5.756 N and a = ΣF / m = −5.756 / 1.2 = −4.80 m/s²: about half of g.
  4. Why this step: the speed stops growing when the drag balances the weight, D = W. v = √(W / k2) = √(11.756 / 0.06) = √195.93 = 14.00 m/s. The drag-free result of step 2, 19.79 m/s, is above this limit, so it cannot be right: the drone never reaches that speed. This limit is the terminal speed, taught in plan row fly.10.

Check your answer. At 14.00 m/s the drag is 0.06 × 14.00² = 11.76 N, which equals the weight 11.756 N to the digits kept.

PROBLEM 4 · A RACING DRONE PUNCHES UP 10 m

A racing drone of mass 0.8 kg has TWR = 4.0 at the home field. It hovers, then goes to full thrust. Ignore the drag and the motor lag (page 2.2c). The notes [69] say that racing drones sometimes reach a TWR of 3 or higher (PDF page 31). Questions. (a) Find the weight and the largest thrust. (b) Find the acceleration at full thrust. (c) Find the time to rise 10.0 m and the speed at that height.

Show solution
  1. Why this step: the TWR gives Tmax from the weight. W = m·g = 0.8 × 9.8062 = 7.845 N and Tmax = TWR × W = 4.0 × 7.845 = 31.380 N.
  2. Why this step: the net force at full thrust is Tmax − W. a = (Tmax − W) / m = (31.380 − 7.845) / 0.8 = 23.535 / 0.8 = 29.42 m/s², which is 3 g.
  3. Why this step: the drone starts from rest with constant acceleration, so Δh = ½·a·t² gives the time. t = √(2·h / a) = √(2 × 10.0 / 29.42) = √0.68 = 0.82 s.
  4. Why this step: the speed is a times t. v = a·t = 29.42 × 0.82 = 24.12 m/s. In a real flight the drag and the motor lag lower this number.

Check your answer. The area of the triangle is ½ × 0.82 × 24.12 = 9.89 m, which is 10.0 m to the digits kept.

PROBLEM 5 · THE HOVER COMMAND MOVES WITH THE MASS

The drone of Try it 11 has TWR = 2.0 empty (mass 1.2 kg), so its hover command is 0.50, as in the default of PX4's MPC_THR_HOVER [29]. The thrust law is linear in the command. It takes a parcel of 0.50 kg (total mass 1.7 kg). Questions. (a) Find the new hover command. (b) Find the new TWR. (c) Find the best vertical acceleration now.

Show solution
  1. Why this step: with a linear law the hover command is the weight divided by Tmax, so it grows in proportion to the mass. u = 0.50 × 1.7 / 1.2 = 0.7083.
  2. Why this step: the TWR is the inverse of the hover command. TWR = 1 / u = 1 / 0.7083 = 1.41.
  3. Why this step: the best acceleration is (TWR − 1)·g. amax = (TWR − 1)·g = (1.41 − 1) × 9.8062 = 4.02 m/s². The parcel took away more than half of the spare climb acceleration (9.806 m/s² empty).

Check your answer. The spare share of the range is 1 − 0.7083 = 0.29, against 0.50 for the empty drone. The spare acceleration falls in a similar way: 4.02 / 9.806 = 0.41 of the empty value.

THINK 1 · HARD · A PARCEL AT A HIGH FIELD

Situation. A delivery drone of mass 1.2 kg has a largest total thrust of 30.0 N at sea-level density. It must carry a parcel of 0.40 kg from Denver (g = 9.7965 m/s², air density 1.049 kg/m³, against 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level). Rotor thrust is in proportion to the air density (2.3.4). Figure 29 shows the thrusts.

Questions. (a) Find the largest thrust in Denver. (b) Find the TWR with the parcel in Denver, and the best climb acceleration. (c) Find the largest payload for TWR 2 in Denver and compare it with the sea-level value of 0.33 kg. (d) Is the cause the lower g or the lower density?

Think first.

  1. Which of the two numbers, g or ρ, changes the thrust, and which changes the weight?
  2. g is smaller in Denver. Does that make the drone's job easier or harder, and by how much?
  3. If the thrust falls by 14 % and the weight falls by 0.1 %, which one decides?
HintScale the thrust by the ratio of the two densities first. Then repeat the TWR steps with the new thrust and the new weight.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: the thrust follows the density, so scale it by the density ratio. ρ(Denver) / ρ(sea level) = 1.049 / 1.225 = 0.856 and Tmax(Denver) = 0.856 × 30.0 = 25.68 N.
  2. Why this step: the weight uses the new mass and the Denver g. m = 1.2 + 0.40 = 1.6 kg and W = m·g = 1.6 × 9.7965 = 15.674 N. Then TWR = 25.68 / 15.674 = 1.64, below 2, and amax = (25.68 − 15.674) / 1.6 = 10.006 / 1.6 = 6.25 m/s².
  3. Why this step: for TWR 2 the mass limit comes from Tmax = 2·m·g. m = 25.68 / (2.0 × 9.7965) = 25.68 / 19.593 = 1.31 kg, so the largest payload is 1.31 − 1.2 = 0.11 kg. At sea level it was 0.33 kg: the high field cost 0.33 − 0.11 = 0.22 kg, two thirds of the payload.
  4. Why this step: test the g effect alone, with the sea-level thrust. m = 30.0 / (2.0 × 9.7965) = 30.0 / 19.593 = 1.53 kg, the same as at sea level. So the lower g changes nothing at this precision. The density is the cause.

Check your answer. At the home field the same parcel gives W = 1.6 × 9.8062 = 15.690 N and TWR = 30.0 / 15.690 = 1.91, already below 2. So the parcel is too heavy at sea level as well, and Denver makes it worse (1.64).

What if … the pilot insists on TWR 2 with the 0.40 kg parcel in Denver?

Show the answerThe drone would need Tmax = 2.0 × 15.674 = 31.348 N in Denver, which is 31.348 / 25.68 = 1.22 times what it has: 22 % more thrust, from larger propellers or a stronger motor, or the parcel must be lighter.
THINK 2 · HARD · ONE ROTOR FAILS

Situation. A quadrotor of mass 1.2 kg has four equal rotors, each with a largest thrust of 6.0 N (so Tmax = 24.0 N). In hover at the home field one rotor stops. This problem looks only at the thrust and the weight. How the drone then turns (chapter 3) is not part of it. Figure 30 draws the rotors.

Questions. (a) Find the TWR with four and with three rotors. (b) Find the thrust each working rotor must make to hold the weight, and its share of 6.0 N. (c) Find the best climb acceleration with three rotors. (d) What payload could the drone still hover with on three rotors?

Think first.

  1. What does the loss of a rotor change in the sum T, and what stays the same in the weight?
  2. Must the three rotors make the same thrust to give T = W?
  3. Does a TWR above 1 guarantee a safe flight?
HintWrite the largest thrust of the working rotors first; then use W = m·g and TWR = Tmax / W.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: the weight is the same in both cases. W = m·g = 1.2 × 9.8062 = 11.767 N. With four rotors TWR = 24.0 / 11.767 = 2.04. With three working rotors the largest thrust is 3 × 6.0 = 18.0 N, so TWR = 18.0 / 11.767 = 1.53.
  2. Why this step: to hold the weight the sum must equal W. Here we split it equally to see the load on each rotor. In a real quadrotor the split cannot stay equal: three equal thrusts no longer cancel each other's tipping torques, and chapter 3 shows that the rotor opposite the failed one must drop to almost nothing. The sum, which is what this page uses, is the same either way. Four rotors: 11.767 / 4 = 2.942 N, a share of 2.942 / 6.0 = 0.49. Three rotors: 11.767 / 3 = 3.922 N, a share of 3.922 / 6.0 = 0.65.
  3. Why this step: the best climb uses all the thrust that is left. amax = (18.0 − 11.767) / 1.2 = 6.233 / 1.2 = 5.19 m/s², against (24.0 − 11.767) / 1.2 = 10.19 m/s² with four rotors.
  4. Why this step: the largest mass that three rotors can hold has Tmax = m·g. m = 18.0 / 9.8062 = 1.84 kg, so the payload is at most 1.84 − 1.2 = 0.64 kg, and no margin is left at that mass.

Check your answer. With three rotors each at 0.65 of the limit, the total is 3 × 3.922 = 11.766 N, which equals the weight to the digits kept.

What if … the drone carries a payload of 0.80 kg (total mass 2.0 kg) when the rotor fails?

Show the answerW = 2.0 × 9.8062 = 19.612 N and ΣF = 18.0 − 19.612 = −1.612 N, so a = −1.612 / 2.0 = −0.81 m/s²: the drone sinks even at full thrust. Spare thrust is what protects against a failure; the TWR rule of the notes gives exactly that.
THINK 3 · HARD · A SPRAY TANK THAT EMPTIES

Situation. A spray drone of mass 1.2 kg carries a tank with 1.0 kg of liquid. It hovers at the home field; the tank empties at 0.25 kg per minute. The pilot sets the thrust for the start and does not touch it afterward. Figure 31 draws the weight and the thrust against time.

Questions. (a) Find the thrust at the start. (b) Find the acceleration after 1 minute and after 2 minutes. (c) Express the acceleration after 2 minutes as a share of g. (d) What hover thrust does the controller need when the tank is empty, as a share of the start value?

Think first.

  1. At the start the drone hovers. What does that say about the thrust?
  2. Does the mass at time t use the full or the remaining liquid?
  3. If the thrust stays constant, what makes the net force?
HintThe thrust is the weight at t = 0. For later times compute the new mass, the new weight, then the net force and divide by the new mass.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: hover means T = W at the start, with the full tank. m0 = 1.2 + 1.0 = 2.2 kg and T = W0 = m·g = 2.2 × 9.8062 = 21.574 N.
  2. Why this step: after 1 minute 0.25 kg has gone; the weight falls and the unchanged thrust is now too large. m = 2.2 − 0.25 = 1.95 kg, W = 1.95 × 9.8062 = 19.122 N, ΣF = 21.574 − 19.122 = 2.452 N and a = ΣF / m = 2.452 / 1.95 = 1.26 m/s².
  3. Why this step: the same steps after 2 minutes. m = 2.2 − 0.50 = 1.70 kg, W = 1.70 × 9.8062 = 16.671 N, ΣF = 21.574 − 16.671 = 4.903 N and a = 4.903 / 1.70 = 2.88 m/s².
  4. Why this step: a share of g shows how big the effect is. 2.88 / 9.8062 = 0.29: after two minutes the drone would climb with 0.29 g if nobody lowered the thrust.
  5. Why this step: with the tank empty the mass is the drone's own 1.2 kg, whose hover thrust is its weight, 11.767 N. The share of the start value is 11.767 / 21.574 = 0.55. The controller must cut the thrust to a little more than half over four minutes.

Check your answer. Each minute removes 0.25 kg, which lowers the weight by 0.25 × 9.8062 = 2.452 N; after the first minute the net force equals exactly this loss, 2.452 N, as in step 2.

What if … the thrust is cut to follow the weight, but the tank is not full at the start (only 0.5 kg of liquid)?

Show the answerThen the start mass is 1.7 kg, the start thrust is 1.7 × 9.8062 = 16.671 N, and the hover thrust of the empty drone is the same 11.767 N. The controller's job is the same, with a smaller range: the hover thrust follows m·g all along.
Where the owner's notes need care. The notes [69] (library item AD139, chapter 2) are the source for the structure of this page. Each claim was checked; the full table with the verdicts is in the knowledge file knowledge/2.3_dynamics.md. The points where the notes differ from this page are:
  • PDF page 27: the drone "experiences a force that is equal and opposite to the force of gravity" is given as an example of the third law. Thrust and weight act on the same body, so they are not a pair (2.3.7). They are equal in hover because the net force is zero.
  • PDF page 28: thrust "is measured in kilograms or newtons". The unit of a force is the newton; the kilogram is a unit of mass (Try it 7).
  • PDF page 28: "lift" is used for the vertical force of the rotors. This course calls that force thrust and keeps "lift" for the force of a wing (plan row fly.7).
  • PDF pages 30 and 31: "at 50 % throttle the total thrust equals the weight" holds only with TWR 2 and a linear law (2.3.4).
  • PDF page 34: "one-fourth of the total force produced by the rotor is consumed in overcoming the drag" is not used. No source we read says it, and the same page's hover equation uses the whole sum of the four thrusts.
  • PDF page 34: the rotation law is written "T = L × a" with the words "torque = inertia multiplied by angular acceleration". The words are right. The letters clash with this page, where T is the thrust and a the linear acceleration, so chapter 3 writes it τ = I·α.
  • PDF page 32: the notes give the rotor speed ω in rpm. The law F = kF·ω² holds in any unit of speed; only the number kF changes (2.3.4).
  • PDF page 36: with θ the tilt of the drone, the notes write the horizontal part as F cos θ and the vertical part as F sin θ. That would give no vertical force in hover (θ = 0). With θ the tilt, the parts are F sin θ (horizontal) and F cos θ (vertical); the notes' form is right only for an angle measured from the horizontal (Problem 2).
