2.1.9 Putting the forces together

Forces add like arrows: put them tip to tail, and the arrow from the first tail to the last tip is the net force.

How to read this table. The table has two rows, one for each possible net force. Read a row from left to right: the net force first, then what the object does, then the law behind it. It matters for flying because every flight state is one of these two rows: hover and steady cruise are the first row, and every climb, dive, turn or change of speed is the second. Fnet is the net force. It is the one arrow you get when you add all the force arrows (thrust, drag, lift, weight) tip to tail. Its length tells how hard all the forces together push, in newtons (N), and its direction tells which way they push. It gets bigger when the forces stop cancelling each other. m is the mass of the object in kilograms (kg); a heavier object has a bigger m. a is the acceleration: how fast the velocity changes, in meters per second squared (m/s²). The velocity is the arrow that says how fast and in which direction the object moves (m/s). The formula a = Fnet / m says that a bigger net force gives a bigger a, and a bigger mass gives a smaller a. Figure 2 draws both rows.

How to read this table. Each row is one fact about splitting a slanted arrow. Read the row name, then the formula, then the sign rule. It matters for flying because a simulator or an autopilot never adds slanted arrows directly: it first splits each arrow into a sideways part and an up part, and then adds all the sideways parts together and all the up parts together. Here is every symbol. F is the size of the arrow you want to split, which means its length, in newtons (N); a stronger force has a longer arrow. α (alpha) is the angle between the arrow and the forward horizontal, turned toward up, in degrees; the bigger α is, the steeper the arrow. Fx is the sideways part: how much of the arrow points forward (the x direction), in N. cos α is the share of F that points forward: it is 1 for a flat forward arrow and 0 for an arrow that points straight up. Fz is the up part: how much of the arrow points up (the z direction), in N. sin α is the share that points up: 0 for a flat arrow and 1 for a straight-up arrow. The sign rule: Fx is + when the arrow has a forward part and − when it has a back part; Fz is + for an up part and − for a down part. If the arrow points back or down, cos α or sin α is negative by itself, so the formulas carry the signs. The last row is a check: Fx and Fz are the two shorter sides of a right triangle, F is its long side, so Pythagoras gives F = √(Fx² + Fz²). Figure 3 draws all three rows: on the left an arrow with two + parts, on the right an arrow with two − parts.

How to read this table. Each row is one of the four forces on the climbing plane. Read across: the force and its letter, which way its arrow points, how much of it points forward (the x part), and how much of it points up (the z part). It matters for flying because a climbing plane is the normal case: none of its forces lines up with the ground except the weight, so you must find the parts before you can add anything. Here is every symbol. T is thrust, the push of the propeller or rotors, in newtons (N); it gets bigger when the motor works harder. Why the signs look like this. Thrust points along the path, so both its parts are + (forward and up). Drag points against the path. The path points forward and up, so the drag points back and down. Back is a negative x and down is a negative z, so both parts of the drag are negative: −D·cos γ and −D·sin γ. Lift stands at a right angle to the path. When the path tilts up by γ, the lift tilts back by the same angle γ: it no longer points straight up but a little backward. That is why its up part shrinks to L·cos γ, and why its x part is negative, −L·sin γ. (With γ = 0 the lift would point straight up and its x part would be 0.) Weight points straight down whatever the path does, so its x part is 0 and its z part is −m·g.

How to read this table. The four rows are the four steps, in the order you do them. In each row the first cell names the step and the second cell gives its formula. It matters for flying because an autopilot or a simulator does exactly this in every time step: it adds the parts, finds how hard and in which direction the plane is pushed, and turns that push into an acceleration. Here is every symbol. Tx, Dx, Lx, Wx are the x parts (sideways parts) of thrust, drag, lift and weight, from the table above; forward is +, back is −. Fx is the x part of the net force: all four sideways parts added with their signs, in newtons (N). It is what is left pushing forward (or back) after the forward and the backward pushes have cancelled as far as they can. Tz, Dz, Lz, Wz are the z parts (up parts) of the four forces; up is +, down is −. Weight points down, so Wz = −m·g. Fz is the z part of the net force: all four up parts added with their signs. It is what is left pushing up (or down). Fnet is the size of the net force. That means the length of the net-force arrow, in newtons: how hard all the forces together push, no matter in which direction. The two parts Fx and Fz are the two shorter sides of a right triangle and Fnet is its long side, so Pythagoras gives Fnet = √(Fx² + Fz²). The direction of the net force is the angle of the long side above the horizontal, arctan(Fz / Fx), as in the last step of Try it 19a. If Fnet is 0 the forces cancel completely. m is the mass of the plane in kg; a heavier plane gets a smaller acceleration from the same push. ax = Fx / m and az = Fz / m are the two parts of the acceleration, in m/s²: how fast the forward speed and the climb speed change. They are the same triangle as the force triangle, with every side divided by m, so the acceleration a = Fnet / m points the same way as the net force. Figure 6 draws the four rows in four panels.

TRY IT 19a · A CLIMBING PLANE, BY PARTS
A 3 kg plane climbs along a path tilted γ = 10° up. Its thrust is 20 N, its drag 8 N, its lift 28 N (home field). Find Fx, Fz, the net force and the acceleration. Does the plane speed up, slow down, or keep its speed?
Show solution
  1. The angle: cos γ = cos 10° = 0.9848, sin γ = sin 10° = 0.1736.
  2. x parts: Tx = T·cos γ = 20 × 0.9848 = 19.696 N; Dx = −D·cos γ = −8 × 0.9848 = −7.878 N; Lx = −L·sin γ = −28 × 0.1736 = −4.861 N; Wx = 0.
  3. Step 1: Fx = Tx + Dx + Lx + Wx = 19.696 − 7.878 − 4.861 + 0 = 6.957 N.
  4. z parts: Tz = T·sin γ = 20 × 0.1736 = 3.472 N; Dz = −D·sin γ = −8 × 0.1736 = −1.389 N; Lz = L·cos γ = 28 × 0.9848 = 27.574 N; Wz = −m·g = −3 × 9.8062 = −29.419 N.
  5. Step 2: Fz = Tz + Dz + Lz + Wz = 3.472 − 1.389 + 27.574 − 29.419 = 0.238 N.
  6. Step 3: Fnet = √(Fx² + Fz²) = √(6.957² + 0.238²) = 6.961 N.
  7. Step 4: a = Fnet / m = 6.961 / 3 = 2.32 m/s², pointing arctan(Fz / Fx) = arctan(0.238 / 6.957) = 1.96° above the horizontal: almost straight ahead.
  8. The up and down parts almost cancel, but a forward part is left: the plane speeds up.

2.1.10 Tilt: how a drone leans, and why it then flies forward

What "tilt" means. To tilt is to lean. A drone tilts when its whole body turns a little forward (or back, or sideways), the way you lean forward before you start to run. Its rotors are fixed to the body, so the rotor axis, and with it the thrust, leans by the same amount.

How to read this table. The table has two rows, each one a word you meet in every drone flight log. Read a row as word, then meaning. It matters for flying because the tilt decides how much of the thrust pushes forward: the more the drone leans, the more of the thrust points sideways instead of up. θ (theta) is the tilt angle, in degrees. It is the angle between the drone's "up" (its rotor axis, which is the direction the thrust points) and straight up. θ = 0° is level, as in hover. θ gets bigger the more the drone leans. Pitch is the name for the lean forward or back: nose down is a forward lean, nose up is a backward lean. When the drone leans only forward or back, pitch and θ are the same size; section 2.4 adds roll and yaw. Figure 7 draws both rows: a level drone, a drone leaning nose down, and a drone leaning nose up, each with its tilt angle θ.

WordMeaning
tilt angle θ (theta)the angle between the drone's "up" (its rotor axis) and straight up. θ = 0° means level, as in hover.

How does a drone tilt itself? It has no flaps. It changes its motor speeds for a moment: the back rotors spin faster and the front rotors slower, so the back rises and the body leans forward. Then the speeds even out again, and the drone stays tilted. (GMU, Quadcopter Dynamics [20, slide 21]: to pitch forward, back thrust > front thrust.)

The drag D is the push of the air against the motion, in N, and it grows as the drone gets faster. The velocity is the arrow that says how fast and in which direction the drone moves, in m/s. WORKED EXAMPLE · HOVER, THEN FORWARD
A quadrotor has mass 0.80 kg. (a) What thrust does it need to hover? (b) It flies level at V = 10 m/s with CD = 1.0 on a frontal area of 0.02 m². Find the drag, the total thrust and the tilt θ.
  1. (a) T = W = m·g = 0.80 × 9.8062 = 7.845 N, and T / 4 = 1.961 N per rotor.
  2. (b) D = ½·ρ·V²·S·CD = ½ × 1.225 × 10² × 0.02 × 1.0 = 1.225 N.
  3. Steady and level: T·cos θ = W = 7.845 N and T·sin θ = D = 1.225 N.
  4. These two parts are the sides of the right triangle, so T = √(7.845² + 1.225²) = √63.04 = 7.940 N.
  5. tan θ = D / W, so θ = arctan(0.1562) = 8.88°.

It needs only 1.2 % more thrust than in hover, tilted by about 9°.

TRY IT 22 · THINK FIRST
The 0.80 kg drone of the worked example flies at 10 m/s and suddenly levels out (θ = 0), still holding its height. What happens to its speed? How fast does it slow at first?
Show solution
  1. With θ = 0 there is no forward part of thrust. The only sideways force left is drag, pointing backward.
  2. So it slows down. Drag at 10 m/s: D = ½·ρ·V²·S·CD = 1.225 N (worked example). Newton's second law: a = D / m = 1.225 / 0.80 = 1.53 m/s² backward at first.

2.1.11 No CD in any table? Measure your own drone's CD

You built a drone, and no table lists its CD. Every way to get it rests on one idea: find the drag D some other way, then divide by q·S. Use the drone's frontal area as S, and fly on a calm day so that airspeed equals ground speed. Read a row from left to right: the name of the way, what you measure, the formula that turns the measurement into CD, and why it works. These are the symbols of the formulas:

  • CD is the drag coefficient, a number without a unit that describes how much drag a shape makes. A blunter, rougher shape has a bigger CD.
  • D is the drag force in newtons (N). Dcomputed is the drag that a computer simulation reports.
  • ρ (rho) is the air density in kg/m³ (about 1.225 at sea level). Thin air has a smaller ρ.
  • V is the speed of the drone through the air, in m/s. Vt is the steady speed of a falling drone at which drag and weight are equal.
  • S is the frontal area in m²: the area you see when you look at the drone from the front (from above for a falling flat frame). Si is the area of part number i, and CD,i is the CD of that part. A bigger S means more air hit and more drag.
  • The group ρ·V²·S in the formulas is twice the drag that CD = 1 would make: it is the pressure of the moving air, q = ½·ρ·V², times S, times 2. So every formula says: CD = D divided by (q·S).
  • m is the mass in kg and g the gravity number (9.8062 m/s² at the home field), so m·g is the weight in N.
  • T is the thrust in N. θ is the tilt angle in degrees, and tan θ is sin θ / cos θ, the forward part of the thrust divided by its up part. It gets bigger as the drone leans more.
  • a is how fast the drone slows down just after you level out, in m/s².

Way 1 · Wind tunnel: measure D directly

The idea. Hold the drone still and move the air past it at a known speed. The drag is then the push of the air on the holder, read on a force balance (Figure 10).

How to read this table. The five rows are the five steps of a wind-tunnel test, in the order you do them. Read a row as step number, what you do, and the formula for it (a dash means there is nothing to calculate). It matters for flying because a wrong ρ or a wrong V gives a wrong CD (Try it 22a). Way 6 · Computer simulation (CFD): let software compute D

The idea. CFD means computational fluid dynamics. The software takes a 3-D model of the drone, fills the air around it with many small cells (a "mesh"), and solves the equations of air flow in every cell. Then it adds up the push of the air on every small patch of the drone's surface: the pressure pushes at a right angle to each patch, and the friction drags along it. The sum along the flow direction is the drag Dcomputed (Figure 17).

How to read this table. The six rows are the six steps of a CFD run, in order. PROBLEM 2 · THINK IN V²
A plane flies level at 20 m/s, then slows to 10 m/s with the same CL. What fraction of its weight does lift now hold? By what factor must CL grow to stay level?
Show solution
  1. Same S, ρ and CL in L = ½·ρ·V²·S·CL, so L2 / L1 = (V2 / V1)² = (10 / 20)² = 0.25: a quarter of the weight, so the plane sinks.
  2. To keep L = W: CL,2 = CL,1·(V1 / V2)², so CL must grow × 4: the pilot raises the nose (more angle of attack, section 2.8), up to the stall.
PROBLEM 3 · HOW FAR CAN IT TILT?
A 2 kg drone can make at most 50 N of thrust. What is the largest tilt at which it can still hold its height?
Show solution
  1. Holding height needs the up part to equal the weight: T·cos θ = W = 19.61 N.
  2. With the most thrust: cos θmax = W / Tmax = 19.61 / 50 = 0.3922.
  3. θmax = arccos(W / Tmax) = arccos(0.3922) = 66.9°. Tilt more, and the up part is too small: the drone sinks.

  2. Per rotor: Trotor = W / 4 = 34.322 / 4 = 8.580 N; share Trotor / Trotor,max = 8.580 / 15 = 57.2 %. Without the parcel: 3.677 / 15 = 24.5 %.
  3. Tmax = 4 × 15 = 60 N; TWR = Tmax / W = 60 / 34.322 = 1.75; without the parcel 60 / 14.709 = 4.08.
  4. a = (Tmax − W) / m = (60 − 34.322) / 3.5 = 7.34 m/s².
  5. With the parcel: cos θmax = W / Tmax = 34.322 / 60 = 0.572, so θ = 55.1°. Without: cos θmax = 14.709 / 60 = 0.245, so θ = 75.8°. The parcel takes away about 21° of tilt, so the drone turns and speeds up much more slowly.
  6. CD·S = Σ CD,i·Si = 1.0 × 0.03 + 1.28 × 0.02 = 0.0556 m². q = ½·ρ·V² = 88.2 Pa, so D = q·(CD·S) = 88.2 × 0.0556 = 4.90 N.
  7. tan θ = D / W = 4.90 / 34.322, so θ = 8.13°. T = √(W² + D²) = √(34.322² + 4.90²) = 34.67 N, or T / 4 = 8.67 N per rotor (57.8 % of 15 N).
  8. The weight falls a little with Denver's g: W = m·g = 3.5 × 9.7965 = 34.288 N, 0.10 % less, so the hover thrust is almost the same. The drag falls with ρ: D2 = D1·ρ2 / ρ1 = 4.90 × 1.049 / 1.225 = 4.20 N. Rotor speed: n2 / n1 − 1 = √( (g2 / g1) · (ρ1 / ρ2) ) − 1 = 8.0 % faster. Full speed now gives only Tmax,2 = Tmax,1·ρ2 / ρ1 = 60 × 1.049 / 1.225 = 51.38 N, so TWR = Tmax,2 / W = 51.38 / 34.288 = 1.50.
PROBLEM 6 · DELIVERY IN A CROSSWIND
The loaded drone of Problem 5 must hover over the drop point while a wind blows from the west at 8 m/s. Find the drag and which way it pushes, how the drone leans, and its thrust.
Show solution
  1. Wind from the west blows toward the east. Hovering, the airspeed is 8 m/s.
  2. D = ½·ρ·V²·(CD·S) = ½ × 1.225 × 8² × 0.0556 = 2.18 N, pushing east.
  3. It leans into the wind, toward the west: tan θ = D / W = 2.18 / 34.322, so θ = 3.63°.
  4. T = √(W² + D²) = √(34.322² + 2.18²) = 34.39 N, and T / W − 1 = only 0.2 % more than in still air. A light wind costs little thrust, but the drone must lean to stay in place.
PROBLEM 7 · A PENTAROTOR WITH A PAYLOAD, HIGH UP
A pentarotor (five rotors) has mass 2.5 kg and carries a 2 kg payload. Its 10-inch propellers (d = 0.254 m) have CT = 0.10. Find the hover rpm at the home field (sea level) and in Denver (h = 1 600 m: ρ = 1.049 kg/m³, g = 9.7965 m/s²).
Show solution
  1. W = m·g = 4.5 × 9.8062 = 44.128 N; per rotor Trotor = W / N = 44.128 / 5 = 8.826 N.
  2. d⁴ = 0.254⁴ = 0.004162 m⁴.
  3. n = √( Trotor / (CT·ρ·d⁴) ) = √(8.826 / (0.10 × 1.225 × 0.004162)) = 131.6 rev/s; rpm = 60·n: about 7 900 rpm.
  4. In Denver: W = m·g = 4.5 × 9.7965 = 44.084 N, so n = √( W / (N·CT·ρ·d⁴) ) = √(44.084 / (5 × 0.10 × 1.049 × 0.004162)) = 142.1 rev/s, about 8 500 rpm.
Large propellers turn slowly: the d⁴ makes each turn count.

  1. A tilted drone flies forward at a steady speed. What is the net force?
    answerZero: the up part of thrust equals the weight, and the forward part equals the drag.

  3. How can you get the CD of a drone you built without a wind tunnel?
    answerFly it level at a steady speed V and read the tilt θ from the log: CD = 2·m·g·tan θ / (ρ·V²·S). Or use the coast-down or the drop test.

★ Summary

How to read this table. Each row is one idea of this page, with the one thing to keep from it. Read the rows from top to bottom: each idea is used by the next one. It matters for flying because these six lines are what you need to compute forces, tilt and drag on a drone, with or without wind, and to read the formulas of a simulator. Figure 32 shows the same six rows as a map in which an arrow means "the next idea uses this one". Here are the symbols, row by row.

  • Net force. Fnet is the size of the net force, the length of the net-force arrow, in newtons (N); m is the mass in kg; a is the acceleration in m/s².
  • Tilted drone. T is the thrust (N), θ the tilt angle in degrees, W the weight (N, W = m·g), D the drag (N). T·cos θ is the up part of the thrust and T·sin θ its forward part.
  • Your own CD. CD is the drag coefficient (no unit); q = ½·ρ·V² is the pressure of the moving air (Pa), with ρ the air density and V the airspeed; S is the frontal area (m²); tan θ is the forward part of the thrust divided by its up part; a in "D = m·a" is how fast the drone slows after you level out.
  • Wind. vg is the ground velocity, vw the wind, V the airspeed, all in m/s.
  • Wind at an angle. β is the angle in the level plane from the flight line to the direction the wind comes from; α is the angle of the wind above or below the level; Vw is the wind speed, Vh its level part and Vv its up/down part (m/s).
  • Simulators. kf is the thrust per square of the rotor speed, ω the rotor speed in rad/s, CT the thrust coefficient, ρ the air density, n the rotor speed in turns per second, D (in "CT·ρ·n²·D⁴") the propeller diameter in m, Tmax the largest thrust of a rotor, u the motor command from 0 to 1, uh the hover command, and BC the ballistic coefficient in kg/m² (the mass divided by the drag area).
