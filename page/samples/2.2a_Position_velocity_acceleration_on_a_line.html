2.2.1 Position, displacement and distance

Think of the straight run between two gates as a line. Choose one point on the line and call it the origin O. Choose one direction along the line and call it plus (+). Usually + points forward.

Position x tells where the drone is. It is the distance from O, measured with a sign: + if the drone is on the plus side of O, − if it is on the other side. The unit is the meter (m). Position gets bigger when the drone moves in the plus direction. Time t is the clock reading in seconds (s); it gets bigger as the flight goes on.

Three words sound alike and mean different things. The example for all of them: gate A is at x1 = 10 m. The drone flies forward to a camera pole at 70 m, turns around, and stops at gate B at x2 = 40 m.

How to read this table. The table has three rows, one for each quantity. Read a row from left to right: the name, the formula, the numbers of the example, and whether the quantity can be negative. It matters for flying because a flight log stores positions, and every other quantity of this page is built from differences of positions. Here is every symbol. x1 is the position where the drone starts and x2 the position where it ends, both in m. Δx (delta x) is the displacement: the change of position, end minus start, in m. It is + when the drone ends further forward than it started and − when it ends further back. d is the distance: the total length of the path the drone flew, in m. The distance adds up the length of every leg of the flight, so it never gets smaller as the flight goes on. xturn is the position where the drone turns around, 70 m in the example. The bars in |x2 − x1| mean "the size without the sign": 3 and −3 both become 3. Figure 2 draws the three rows, one below the other.

Use displacement when you want "where am I compared with where I started", and distance when you want "how far did the motors carry me" (for example, for a battery estimate).

TRY IT 2a · MEDIUM · THE SAME FLIGHT FROM TWO ORIGINS

Gate A and gate B are 60 m apart on a straight run. A drone starts 10 m from gate A toward gate B and flies toward gate B until it is 50 m from gate A. It turns around and stops 35 m from gate A. Operator 1 puts the origin at gate A and the plus direction toward gate B. Operator 2 puts the origin at gate B and the plus direction toward gate A. Figure 4 draws both. Each operator writes the start position x1, the end position x2, the displacement Δx and the distance d. Which of these four numbers differ between the two operators? Does one operator describe a different flight?

Show solution
  1. Why this step: operator 1 reads the positions straight from the distance to gate A. x1 = 10 m and x2 = 35 m, so Δx = x2 − x1 = 35 − 10 = +25 m. The legs have the sizes |50 − 10| = 40 m and |35 − 50| = 15 m, so d = 40 + 15 = 55 m.
  2. Why this step: operator 2 measures from gate B, with the plus direction reversed. A point that is x from gate A is 60 − x from gate B, so x′ = 60 − x. The start is x′1 = 60 − 10 = 50 m and the end is x′2 = 60 − 35 = 25 m.
  3. Why this step: the displacement follows from the new positions. Δx′ = x′2 − x′1 = 25 − 50 = −25 m. The turn is at 60 − 50 = 10 m in this frame, so the legs have the sizes |10 − 50| = 40 m and |25 − 10| = 15 m, and d′ = 40 + 15 = 55 m.
  4. Why this step: compare the two descriptions. The positions differ, because the origin moved. The displacement has the opposite sign, because the plus direction is reversed: −25 = −(+25). The distance is the same, 55 m, because the legs have the same lengths in any frame. It is one flight, described twice.

Check your answer. A point has the two coordinates x and x′ that add up to the gate distance: 10 + 50 = 60 m for the start and 35 + 25 = 60 m for the end.

What if … operator 3 keeps the origin at gate A but points the plus direction away from gate B?

Show the answerNow the whole flight is on the minus side: x1 = −10 m and x2 = −35 m, so Δx = −35 − (−10) = −25 m. The sign of Δx follows the plus direction, not the origin, and d is still 55 m.
Trap: a negative displacement does not mean "the drone went the wrong way". It means that the plus direction of that operator points the other way.
TRY IT 2b · HARD · WHERE DID THE DRONE TURN?

Situation. A flight computer stores only three numbers for a straight run: the first position x1 = 10 m, the last position x2 = 40 m, and the odometer reading d = 130 m, the whole length of the path. The drone turned around exactly once, and its first leg went forward. The log does not say where the turn was. Figure 5, in the solution, draws the answer.

Questions. (a) Where did the drone turn? (b) Could the same three numbers come from a flight with two turns? (c) Could the odometer have read 25 m? (d) When is the distance equal to the size of the displacement?

Think first.

  1. The drone flew forward and then back. Which of the two legs is longer?
  2. Call the position of the turn xturn. How do you write the two legs with it?
  3. The distance adds sizes; the displacement adds signed steps. Which one can be smaller?
HintThe forward leg runs from x1 to xturn, and the back leg runs from xturn to x2. Add their lengths and set the sum equal to 130 m.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: with one turn and a forward first leg, the distance is the forward leg plus the back leg. d = (xturn − x1) + (xturn − x2) = 2·xturn − 10 − 40 = 130.
  2. Why this step: solve for the one unknown. 2·xturn = 130 + 50 = 180, so xturn = 90 m.
  3. Why this step: for (b), notice what the extra distance is. The displacement is Δx = 40 − 10 = +30 m. Every meter that the drone flies against the net direction must be flown forward again. So d = Δx + 2 × (distance flown backward), and 130 = 30 + 2 × 50. The drone flew 50 m backward, however many turns it made. A flight with two turns can do this: 10 m → 75 m → 25 m → 40 m. Forward 65 m, back 50 m, forward 15 m: 65 + 50 + 15 = 130 m and 65 − 50 + 15 = +30 m. So yes: the three numbers cannot tell one turn from two.
  4. Why this step: for (c), compare d with the size of Δx. The distance adds the sizes of the legs. The displacement adds the same legs with their signs. A sum of signed numbers is never larger in size than the sum of their sizes, so d ≥ |Δx|. With |Δx| = 30 m, a reading of 25 m is impossible.
  5. Why this step: for (d), the two are equal only when no leg cancels another leg. That is the case when all legs point the same way, that is, when the drone never turns back. Then d = |Δx|.

Check your answer. Legs of the one-turn flight: forward 90 − 10 = 80 m, back 90 − 40 = 50 m, and 80 + 50 = 130 m, as the odometer says. The end is 10 + 80 − 50 = 40 m, as the log says.

What if … the first leg went backward, with one turn?

Show the answerThe legs are (10 − xturn) back and (40 − xturn) forward, so 50 − 2·xturn = 130, and xturn = −40 m. The drone turned 40 m behind the origin. Check: (10 − (−40)) + (40 − (−40)) = 50 + 80 = 130 m.

2.2.2 Velocity and speed: average and instantaneous

Velocity v tells how fast the position changes and in which direction. The unit is meters per second (m/s). A plus sign means "moving forward", a minus sign "moving backward". Speed is the size of the velocity, |v|, with no sign. A drone that flies backward at 5 m/s has the velocity v = −5 m/s and the speed 5 m/s. (Dellaert & Hutchinson, Introduction to Robotics and Perception [2, §7.2.2.1] (section 7.2.2.1 is called "Positional Kinematics"), write the same link between the two in their drone chapter: the time derivative of the position is the linear velocity, roboticsbook.org/S72_drone_actions.html.)

The average velocity over a time span uses only the start and the end: vavg = Δx / Δt, where Δt = t2 − t1 is the length of the time span in seconds. The average speed uses the distance instead: d / Δt.

The example continues. The drone flew from 10 m to 70 m in the first 6 s (leg 1), then from 70 m back to 40 m in the next 6 s (leg 2). Figure 6 is the graph of its position against time.

How to read this table. The table has five rows. Each row is one velocity or speed of the 12 s trip. Read a row from left to right: what is measured, the formula, the numbers, and the sign. It matters for flying because a flight log gives positions at the clock times, and you must choose whether you want the average over a long span or the value at one moment. Here is every symbol. vavg is the average velocity in m/s; it is bigger when the displacement is bigger or the time is shorter. Δx is the displacement in m and Δt the time span in s. d is the distance in m. v alone means the velocity at one moment, in m/s. The slope of a graph is the rise divided by the run, that is, the change of the up-down value divided by the change of the left-right value; on a graph of x against t the slope has the unit m/s. |v| is the size of v, the speed. Why the average hides the turn. The average velocity, 2.5 m/s, is smaller than both the speed of leg 1 (10 m/s) and the size of the velocity of leg 2 (5 m/s). It uses only the start and the end. The average speed, 7.5 m/s, uses the whole path and is the one that tells how hard the motors worked.

Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one moment. To find it, take an average velocity over a shorter and shorter time span that starts at that moment. The averages come closer and closer to one number, and that number is the instantaneous velocity. On the graph, it is the slope of the tangent: the straight line that just touches the curve at that moment.

Example: a drone starts from rest at x = 0 and its position follows x = t², with x in meters and t in seconds. We look at the moment t = 2 s, where x = 4 m.

Figure 8 is the graph of x against t for a drone that shuttles along the straight run for 10 s. The line has three straight parts, and its corners lie on the grid lines. Questions. (a) Read the velocity in each part. (b) Find the average velocity and the average speed of the whole 10 s. Show solution
  1. Why this step: on a graph of x against t, the velocity of a straight part is its slope: the rise divided by the run, read between two corners. The corners are (0 s, 20 m), (2 s, 50 m), (6 s, 20 m) and (10 s, 40 m). Part 1: v = (50 − 20) / (2 − 0) = +15 m/s. Part 2: v = (20 − 50) / (6 − 2) = −7.5 m/s.   2. Why this step: the average velocity uses only the first and the last point. vavg = Δx / Δt = (40 − 20) / 10 = +2.0 m/s.
  3. Why this step: the average speed needs the distance, so add the size of every part. d = |50 − 20| + |20 − 50| + |40 − 20| = 30 + 30 + 20 = 80 m, and d / Δt = 80 / 10 = 8.0 m/s.
  4. Why this step: the pilot gives every part the same weight, but the parts last 2 s, 4 s and 4 s. The plain mean is (15 − 7.5 + 5) / 3 = 12.5 / 3 = 4.17 m/s, which is wrong. A mean that weights each velocity by its time gives the right value: (15 × 2 − 7.5 × 4 + 5 × 4) / 10 = (30 − 30 + 20) / 10 = 2.0 m/s, the same as step 2.

Trap: the mean of the velocities of the parts is not the average velocity, unless the parts last equally long. The average velocity is always the displacement divided by the whole time.

TRY IT 5b · HARD · VELOCITY FROM A NOISY POSITION LOG

Situation. A flight computer logs the position of a drone 50 times per second, so two log entries are 0.02 s apart. Each logged position is wrong by at most σ = 0.02 m. The drone speeds up with a constant acceleration a = 4 m/s². The computer estimates the velocity at the time t from two logged positions: vest = (x(t + Δt) − x(t)) / Δt. It chooses Δt as a whole number of log steps: 1 step is 0.02 s, 7 steps are 0.14 s, 50 steps are 1.00 s. Figure 10, in the solution, draws the error against Δt.

Questions. (a) With perfect readings, by how much is vest wrong? (b) With readings that are off by at most σ, how large can the extra error be? (c) Add the two errors. Which number of steps gives the smallest bound, and how large is it? (d) Compare 1 step and 50 steps.

Think first.

  1. A very short Δt makes the division by Δt large. What does that do to a reading error?
  2. A very long Δt covers a stretch in which the velocity changes. What does that do to the estimate?
  3. One error falls and the other grows as Δt grows. What does that suggest for the best Δt?
HintFor a constant a, write x(t) = x0 + v0·t + ½·a·t², and subtract x(t) from x(t + Δt).
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: for (a), the difference of two perfect positions is a known expression. x(t + Δt) − x(t) = v0·Δt + ½·a·(2·t·Δt + Δt²) = (v0 + a·t)·Δt + ½·a·Δt². The velocity at t is v = v0 + a·t, so dividing by Δt gives vest = v + ½·a·Δt. The estimate is too large by exactly a·Δt / 2. For Δt = 0.14 s this is 4 × 0.14 / 2 = 0.28 m/s.
  2. Why this step: for (b), each reading is off by at most σ. In the worst case one reading is σ too high and the other σ too low, so the difference is off by at most 2σ. After the division by Δt, the extra error is at most 2σ / Δt. For Δt = 0.14 s this is 2 × 0.02 / 0.14 = 0.286 m/s.
  3. Why this step: for (c), the worst case adds the two errors: E(Δt) = 2σ / Δt + a·Δt / 2. One step: E = 2 × 0.02 / 0.02 + 4 × 0.02 / 2 = 2.00 + 0.04 = 2.04 m/s. Fifty steps: E = 2 × 0.02 / 1.00 + 4 × 1.00 / 2 = 0.04 + 2.00 = 2.04 m/s. Both ends of the range are bad, for opposite reasons.
  4. Why this step: the sum of a falling term p/Δt and a growing term q·Δt is smallest where the two terms are equal. The reason is the square (√(p/Δt) − √(q·Δt))² ≥ 0, which gives p/Δt + q·Δt ≥ 2·√(p·q), with equality when p/Δt = q·Δt. Here p = 2σ = 0.04 and q = a/2 = 2. Equal terms mean Δt² = 4σ / a = 4 × 0.02 / 4 = 0.02, so Δt = 0.141 s. The smallest bound is 2 × √(0.04 × 2) = 2 × √0.08 = 0.566 m/s.
  5. Why this step: the log allows only whole steps, and 0.141 / 0.02 = 7.05, so compare 6, 7 and 8 steps. Six steps: 2 × 0.02 / 0.12 + 4 × 0.12 / 2 = 0.333 + 0.240 = 0.573 m/s. Seven steps: 2 × 0.02 / 0.14 + 4 × 0.14 / 2 = 0.286 + 0.280 = 0.566 m/s. Eight steps: 2 × 0.02 / 0.16 + 4 × 0.16 / 2 = 0.250 + 0.320 = 0.570 m/s. The best choice is 7 steps.

Check your answer. At the best Δt the two terms are about equal: 2 × 0.02 / 0.141 = 0.284 m/s and 4 × 0.141 / 2 = 0.282 m/s. Seven steps cut the bound by 2.04 / 0.566 = 3.6 times compared with one step. The bound is a worst case; a typical error is smaller.

2.2.3 Acceleration

Acceleration a tells how fast the velocity changes. The unit is meters per second squared (m/s²), which you can read as "meters per second, per second". The average acceleration over a time span is aavg = Δv / Δt, where Δv = v2 − v1 is the change of velocity in m/s. The instantaneous acceleration is the slope of the graph of v against t, in the same way that the instantaneous velocity is the slope of the graph of x against t.

The sign of a is easy to misread. A plus sign does not mean "speeding up", and a minus sign does not mean "slowing down". What matters is whether a and v have the same sign.

How to read this table. The table has four rows, one for each combination of the signs of v and a. Read a row from left to right: the sign of v, the sign of a, what the drone does, and a numeric example. It matters for flying because braking before a gate is an acceleration with the opposite sign of the velocity, and a log shows it as a negative a. Here is every symbol. v1 is the velocity at the start of the span and v2 the velocity at its end, both in m/s. Δt is the length of the span in s. a = (v2 − v1) / Δt is the average acceleration in m/s²; a bigger velocity change in the same time gives a bigger a. "Speeds up" means that the speed |v| gets bigger; "slows down" means that it gets smaller. 2.2.5 Four equations for constant acceleration

When the acceleration does not change, the graphs of section 2.2.4 are simple shapes, and you can write the answer as a formula. We use these letters: t is the time since the start, in s. a is the constant acceleration, in m/s². v0 is the velocity at the start (t = 0), in m/s. v is the velocity at the time t, in m/s. x0 is the position at the start, in m. x is the position at the time t, in m. So x − x0 is the displacement in the time t.

Where the equations come from. Use the graph of v against t. With constant a, it is a straight line that starts at v0 and rises (or falls) by a each second.

  1. Equation 1 (the slope). The slope of the line is a, so after the time t the velocity changed by a·t: v = v0 + a·t.
  2. Equation 4 (the area as a trapezoid). The displacement is the area under the line from 0 to t. That region is a trapezoid with the parallel sides v0 and v, and the width t. Its area is the average of the two heights times the width: x = x0 + ½·(v0 + v)·t.
  3. Equation 2 (the area as a rectangle plus a triangle). Cut the same region into a rectangle of height v0 and a triangle on top of it. The rectangle has the area v0·t. The triangle has the height v − v0 = a·t and the width t, so its area is ½·a·t². Together: x = x0 + v0·t + ½·a·t².
  4. Equation 3 (no time). Solve equation 1 for the time, t = (v − v0) / a, and put it into equation 4: x − x0 = ½·(v0 + v)·(v − v0) / a = (v² − v0²) / (2a). Moving the 2a to the other side gives v² = v0² + 2·a·(x − x0).

How to read this table. The table has three rows, one for each phase, and a last row for the sum. Read a row from left to right: the acceleration, the velocity at the start and at the end of the phase, the time, and the distance. It matters for flying because a lap time is the sum of such phases, and the distance of the three phases must add up to the distance between the gates. Here is every symbol. a is the acceleration in m/s². v₀ and v are the velocity at the start and at the end of the phase, in m/s. t is the time of the phase in s; a longer phase has a bigger t. Δx is the distance flown in the phase, in m. Phase 1 uses equation 1 for the time and equation 4 for the distance; phase 3 does the same. Phase 2 has a = 0, so its distance is what remains, 100 − 45.0 − 32.0 = 23.0 m, and its time is that distance divided by the speed, t = Δx / v. ttotal is the sum of the three times. vavg = Δxtotal / ttotal is the average velocity of the whole run. Braking distance

The most useful special case of equation 3 is braking to a stop. The final velocity is v = 0, so 0 = v₀² + 2·a·(x − x₀), and with a negative a the stopping distance is dstop = v₀² / (2·|a|). The stopping time follows from equation 1: tstop = v₀ / |a|. Here |a| is the size of the braking acceleration (no sign), in m/s²; a stronger brake means a bigger |a| and a shorter distance.

How to read this table. The table has three rows, one for each starting speed. Read a row from left to right: the starting speed v₀, the stopping time, and the stopping distance. The braking acceleration is the same in all rows, |a| = 8.0 m/s². It matters for flying because the distance grows with the square of the speed: you cannot judge a stop from the speed alone. Here is every symbol. v₀ is the speed when braking starts, in m/s. tstop = v₀ / |a| is the time to stop, in s. dstop = v₀² / (2·|a|) is the distance to stop, in m. TRY IT 18 · BRAKING DISTANCE
A drone flies at 30 m/s and brakes at 10 m/s² to a stop. Find the stopping distance and the stopping time.
Show solution
  1. dstop = v₀² / (2·|a|) = 30² / (2 × 10) = 900 / 20 = 45 m.
  2. tstop = v₀ / |a| = 30 / 10 = 3.0 s.
TRY IT 19 · BRAKING BEFORE A GATE
A drone flies at 24 m/s. It must pass a gate 40 m ahead at 8 m/s. What constant acceleration does it need, and how long does it take?
Show solution
  1. The time is not known and not wanted, so use equation 3 solved for a: a = (v² − v₀²) / (2·(x − x₀)) = (8² − 24²) / (2 × 40) = (64 − 576) / 80 = −6.4 m/s².
  2. t = (v − v₀) / a = (8 − 24) / (−6.4) = 2.5 s.
  3. Check with equation 4: x − x₀ = ½·(v₀ + v)·t = ½ × (24 + 8) × 2.5 = 40 m.
TRY IT 20 · TIME TO REACH A GATE
A drone starts at rest and accelerates at 5.0 m/s². How long does it need to reach a gate 40 m ahead, and how fast is it then?
Show solution
  1. Equation 2 with v₀ = 0: x − x₀ = ½·a·t², so t = √( 2·(x − x₀) / a ) = √( 2 × 40 / 5.0 ) = √16 = 4.0 s.
  2. v = v₀ + a·t = 0 + 5.0 × 4.0 = 20 m/s.
TRY IT 21 · THE DRONE STOPS FIRST
A drone flies at 20 m/s toward a gate 30 m ahead. Its strongest brake is 8.0 m/s². Does it pass the gate? What does equation 3 say?
Show solution
  1. Equation 3 for the velocity at the gate: v² = v₀² + 2·a·(x − x₀) = 20² + 2 × (−8.0) × 30 = 400 − 480 = −80.
  2. A square cannot be negative. The meaning: the drone reaches v = 0 before it reaches the gate.
  3. Stopping distance: dstop = v₀² / (2·|a|) = 400 / 16 = 25 m. It stops 30 − 25 = 5 m before the gate. (A real drone would not fly backward; equation 3 only describes the motion while the acceleration keeps acting.)

TRY IT 23 · AVERAGE VELOCITY OF THE RUN
The run of the table takes 5.958 s for 100 m. Why is its average velocity (16.78 m/s) smaller than the cruise speed (24.0 m/s) but bigger than the average of the start and end speeds (15 m/s)?
Show solution

THINK 3 · THE BRAKE COMMAND ARRIVES LATE

Situation. A drone flies at 24.0 m/s toward a gate. The gate may be passed at 8.0 m/s at most. The flight computer sends the brake command when the drone is 35.0 m from the gate. The brake starts to work 0.25 s after the command, and the drone keeps its speed during these 0.25 s. After that, it brakes with its strongest constant acceleration, −8.0 m/s². Figure 20 draws the speed against the distance flown since the command.

Questions. (a) How fast does the drone pass the gate? (b) At the latest, how far before the gate must the command be sent? (c) If the command is sent at 35.0 m, which constant acceleration would the drone need after the delay, and can it make it?

Think first.

  1. What does the drone do during the delay, and how far does it go?
  2. You know a distance and two speeds, but not the time. Which equation fits?
  3. What does a brake that is "too weak" look like in the numbers?
HintCut the 35.0 m into a delay part and a braking part. The speed is constant in the first part.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: during the delay the speed is constant, so the distance is speed times time. Δx = v·t = 24.0 × 0.25 = 6.0 m.
  2. Why this step: only the rest of the distance is used for braking. 35.0 − 6.0 = 29.0 m.
  3. Why this step: equation 3 gives the speed at the gate from the distance, without the time. v² = v₀² + 2·a·Δx = 24.0² + 2 × (−8.0) × 29.0 = 576 − 464 = 112, so v = √112 = 10.58 m/s. This is above 8.0 m/s.
  4. Why this step: the latest command distance is the braking distance from 24.0 to 8.0 m/s plus the delay distance. Δx = (8.0² − 24.0²) / (2 × (−8.0)) = (64 − 576) / (−16) = 32.0 m, and 32.0 + 6.0 = 38.0 m.
  5. Why this step: for (c), solve equation 3 for the acceleration with the 29.0 m that are left. a = (v² − v₀²) / (2·Δx) = (8.0² − 24.0²) / (2 × 29.0) = (64 − 576) / 58.0 = −8.83 m/s². Its size is more than 8.0, the strongest brake, so the drone cannot make it.

Check your answer. The command at 35.0 m is 38.0 − 35.0 = 3.0 m too late. At 24.0 m/s, 3.0 m take 3.0 / 24.0 = 0.125 s, half of the delay. A passing speed of 10.58 m/s (above 8.0) and a needed brake of 8.83 m/s² (above 8.0) tell the same story.

What if … the delay were only 0.10 s?

Show the answerThe delay distance is 24.0 × 0.10 = 2.4 m, and the total need is 32.0 + 2.4 = 34.4 m, which is less than 35.0 m. The braking distance is 32.6 m, so v² = 24.0² + 2 × (−8.0) × 32.6 = 576 − 521.6 = 54.4 and v = √54.4 = 7.38 m/s. The drone passes the gate slowly enough.
THINK 4 · A RUN TOO SHORT FOR THE TOP SPEED

Situation. A drone must fly 60 m from rest at gate A to rest at gate B. It speeds up at +6.0 m/s² and brakes at −9.0 m/s². Its speed limit is 24.0 m/s. The plan has no cruise: the drone brakes at the moment where the braking distance is exactly what is left. Figure 21 draws the speed against the time.

Questions. (a) Can the drone reach 24.0 m/s on this run? (b) What peak speed does it reach? (c) How long does the run take?

Think first.

  1. How much track do a speed-up to 24.0 m/s and a stop from 24.0 m/s need together?
  2. If the track is too short, which quantity is now unknown, and which two distances share it?
  3. Should the answer to (b) be below or above the limit?
HintWrite the speed-up distance and the braking distance with equation 3, both with the same unknown peak speed v. Their sum is 60 m.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: test the limit first. If it fits, the answer is easy. Speed-up to 24.0 m/s: 24.0² / (2 × 6.0) = 576 / 12 = 48.0 m. Braking from 24.0 m/s: 24.0² / (2 × 9.0) = 576 / 18 = 32.0 m. Together 80.0 m, which is more than 60 m, so no.
  2. Why this step: without a cruise, the two distances fill the whole run, and both contain the same unknown peak speed v. v² / 12 + v² / 18 = v² × 5 / 36 = 60, so v² = 60 × 36 / 5 = 432.
  3. Why this step: take the root. v = √432 = 20.785 m/s. This is below the limit of 24.0 m/s, as the first step told us.
  4. Why this step: the time of each part is the peak speed divided by the size of its acceleration. T = 20.785 / 6.0 + 20.785 / 9.0 = 3.464 + 2.309 = 5.773 s.

Check your answer. The two distances must add up to the run: 432 / 12 + 432 / 18 = 36.0 + 24.0 = 60.0 m. The speed goes up and down along straight lines, so the average speed is half of the peak: 60 / 5.773 = 10.39 m/s, and 20.785 / 2 = 10.39 m/s.

What if … the run were longer? How long must it be so that the drone just reaches 24.0 m/s?

Show the answerThe track must hold both distances of step 1: 48.0 + 32.0 = 80.0 m. On longer runs the drone cruises at 24.0 m/s between the two parts.
