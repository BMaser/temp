2.1.1 What is a force?

A force is a push or a pull. Every force has two things:

How to read this table. The table has two rows, one for each thing a force has. Read a row from left to right: the part, what it means, and one example. It matters for flying because a drone is steered by forces alone, and a force with a size but no direction (or a direction but no size) cannot tell you where the drone will go. Size is how hard the force pushes. It is measured in newtons (N), and it is bigger when the push is stronger. Direction is the way the force pushes, for example straight down or to the left. In Figure 2 the size is the length of the arrow and the direction is where the arrowhead points.

PartMeaningExample
sizehow hard it pushes5 newtons
directionwhich way it pushesstraight down

Something with a size and a direction is a vector. We draw it as an arrow: the length shows the size, the arrowhead shows the direction.

The unit of force is the newton (N). One newton speeds up 1 kg by 1 m/s every second, so 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². To feel it: a 100 g apple weighs about 1 N (W = m·g = 0.1 × 9.8062 = 0.98 N).

Why a drone pilot cares. A drone cannot grab anything; the only things that act on it are forces. Hover, climb, brake, turn: each one is a choice of forces. A flight controller is, at heart, a machine that picks the rotor forces every few milliseconds. So the whole course rests on one skill: draw the forces as arrows, then add them.

How to read this table. The table has one row for each force. It says who makes the force and which way it points. Read a row from left to right: the force and its letter, the thing that makes it, and its direction. It matters for flying because every flight state is a balance of these four rows, and a drone can change only the forces that its own machines make. W is the weight, in newtons (N); it grows with the mass. T is the thrust, in N; it grows when the propellers push more air. D is the drag, in N; it grows with speed. L is the lift, in N; it grows with speed and with the angle of the wing to the air. Figure 4 below draws all four forces on a plane and on a quadrotor. 2.1.2 Mass m, gravity g, and weight W

These three are easy to mix up. Keep them apart:

How to read this table. The table has one row for each letter. Read a row from left to right: the letter, its name, what it is, its unit, and whether it changes on another planet. It matters for flying because the rotors must carry the weight W, not the mass m, and W is the one that changes with the place. m is the mass, in kilograms (kg): how much matter the drone has. It grows when you add a battery or a camera. g is the gravity, in meters per second squared (m/s²): how fast a falling object speeds up. It is bigger on a planet with stronger gravity. W is the weight, in newtons (N): the pull of gravity on the mass. It grows when either m or g grows. Figure 6 draws m and W on Earth and on Mars. Figure 5 draws the three letters side by side with the numbers of that figure.

W = m · g

When is W bigger? When the mass grows (a bigger battery, a camera, a parcel), or when gravity is stronger (another planet). The drone can never switch its weight off, so thrust or lift must answer it every second of the flight.

Why keep m and W apart? Mass decides how hard it is to change the drone's speed (a = F/m, 2.1.9). Weight decides how much thrust it needs just to stay up. A heavy parcel hurts twice: more weight to carry, and more mass to push around in every turn.

TRY IT 2

Show solution

From weight to the accelerometer: what an IMU reads

Later the autopilot learns how the drone moves from an IMU, a small box with gyroscopes and accelerometers (plan row 12.2, Camera + IMU together, in chapter 12). That chapter needs one fact from this section: an accelerometer does not read the acceleration of the drone. Barfoot [55, §7.4.4, p. 273-274, eq. 7.150] gives its reading as a = C·(r̈ − g). Here r̈ is the true acceleration of the drone in m/s², g is the gravity vector in m/s² (it points down), and C is the rotation that turns the world axes into the sensor axes. He gives the reason: accelerometers typically use test masses as part of the measurement principle. He adds: in free fall, the accelerometers will measure a = 0, whereas at rest, they will measure only gravity (in the sensor frame).

In this course z points up, so the gravity vector is g = (0, 0, −g), with g = 9.8062 m/s² at the home field. A drone that rests has r̈ = 0, so the reading along z is +g: the sensor reads the push that holds the drone up, and that push is the weight W = m·g of this section. A drone in free fall has r̈ = g, so the reading is 0. Multiply the reading by the mass m and you get the force that is not gravity, which is the thrust when drag is ignored (a = F/m, 2.1.9 on page 2.1b). Solà [54, eq. 231, p. 55] writes the same reading with a bias and noise added, am = Rᵀ·(at − g) + ab + an. Hager & Bryne [60, §2.2, after eq. 4] call the bias-corrected reading the specific force. Page 2.2b (2.2.11, step 1) reads the line of PX4 that uses it. Figure 7 draws three cases for the 1.5 kg drone of Try it 2a.

TRY IT 2a · WHAT DOES THE ACCELEROMETER READ?
The 1.5 kg drone of Try it 2 (home field, g = 9.8062 m/s², z up, drag ignored) carries an accelerometer. What does it read along z when the drone (a) rests on a table, (b) hovers, (c) climbs with the acceleration +3.0 m/s², (d) is in free fall after the motors stop? (e) For (c), which thrust does the reading show?
Show solution
  1. At rest the true acceleration is 0, so reading = r̈z − gz = 0 − (−9.8062) = 9.8062 m/s², upward.
  2. A hover is also r̈z = 0, so the reading is again 9.8062 m/s². The accelerometer cannot tell a hover from a rest.
  3. reading = r̈z − gz = 3.0 − (−9.8062) = 12.8062 m/s².
  4. In free fall r̈z = gz = −9.8062, so reading = −9.8062 − (−9.8062) = 0 m/s². The drone is falling, yet the sensor reads nothing.
  5. The force that is not gravity is the thrust: T = m × reading = 1.5 × 12.8062 = 19.209 N. Check with Newton: T = W + m·a = 14.709 + 1.5 × 3.0 = 14.709 + 4.5 = 19.209 N.

How big is g where you fly? Five versions of one formula

g is not the same everywhere on Earth. It is about 0.53 % larger at the poles than at the equator, and it gets a little smaller as you climb. Three causes:

How to read this table. The table has one row for each cause that makes g differ from place to place. Read a row from left to right: the cause, what happens physically, and how big the effect is. It matters for flying because these three causes decide which of the five formulas below you need: near sea level the spin and the shape matter, and high above the ground the height matters. Here is what each symbol means. a is the radius of the Earth at the equator, in meters (m): the distance from the center to the equator. b is the radius at a pole, also in m; it is smaller than a because the Earth is squashed. ω (omega) is the spin rate of the Earth in radians per second (rad/s): how fast it turns, and it is bigger on a planet that turns faster. ω·a is the speed of the ground at the equator, in m/s, because the ground travels a circle of radius a. ω²·a is the outward push that this circle gives, in m/s². It is bigger when the spin is faster or the circle is bigger, and it is zero at the poles. a − b is how much farther the equator is from the center than a pole is, here in kilometers (km). The letters:

  • g is the gravity, in m/s²: how fast a falling object speeds up. It is bigger near the poles and smaller high up.
  • g0 is standard gravity, 9.80665 m/s². It is a number that was fixed by agreement, not measured at your place.
  • R is the radius of the Earth taken as a perfect ball, 6 371 000 m. h is your height above the ground, in m. The ratio R / (R + h) is smaller than 1 and shrinks as h grows, so g gets smaller as you climb.
  • φ (phi) is your latitude, the angle north or south of the equator, in degrees. The sin²φ in the formulas is the sine of that angle, squared: 0 at the equator and 1 at a pole. The letters ge, k, e², a, f and m are fixed numbers of the Earth model; the table of letters just below explains each one.
  • Δg (delta g) is a small correction, in m/s², read from a gravity map (the Earth Gravitational Model 2008, EGM2008 [15]), for the mountains and dense rock under you.

TRY IT 3a · LATITUDE, LIKE PX4 (VERSION 3)
Use version 3 to find g at the equator (φ = 0°), at φ = 45° and at a pole (φ = 90°). How much more does a 1.5 kg drone weigh at the pole than at the equator?
Show solution
  1. Equator, sin 0° = 0: g = ge · (1 + 0) / √(1 − 0) = 9.7803253359 × 1 / 1 = 9.780 m/s².
  2. φ = 45°, s = sin²45° = 0.5: g = ge · (1 + k·s) / √(1 − e²·s) = 9.7803253359 × (1 + 0.000965926) / √(1 − 0.00334719) = 9.806 m/s².
  3. Pole, s = sin²90° = 1: g = ge · (1 + k) / √(1 − e²) = 9.7803253359 × 1.001931853 / √(1 − 0.00669438) = 9.832 m/s².
  4. ΔW = m·(gp − ge) = 1.5 × 0.0519 = 0.078 N, or gp / ge − 1 = 0.53 %.
  5. PX4's code stores k = 0.001931851353 and e = 0.0818191908425 (e² = 0.00669438) [29]: the same model, with k rounded in its 9th decimal place.
TRY IT 3b · THE FULL FORMULA IN DENVER (VERSION 4)
Find g in Denver (φ = 39.74°, h = 1 600 m), step by step. Then find the weight of a 1.5 kg drone there.
Show solution
  1. s = sin²φ = sin²39.74° = 0.40871.
  2. Latitude: 1 + k·s = 1.000790 and 1 − e²·s = 0.997264, so g(φ) = ge · (1 + k·s) / √(1 − e²·s) = 9.7803253359 × 1.000790 / √0.997264 = 9.8015 m/s².
  3. Height: 1 + f + m − 2f·s = 1 + 0.00335281 + 0.00344979 − 2 × 0.00335281 × 0.40871 = 1.004062, so (2/a) · 1.004062 = 3.1484 × 10⁻⁷ per metre; times h: 3.1484 × 10⁻⁷ × 1600 = 0.000504. The square term: 3·h² / a² = 0.0000002.
  4. Fh = 1 − 0.000504 + 0.0000002 = 0.999496.
  5. g(φ, h) = g(φ) · Fh = 9.8015 × 0.999496 = 9.7965 m/s².
  6. W = m·g = 1.5 × 9.7965 = 14.695 N.

  1. The leftover the estimator takes for real motion: a = gassumed − gtrue = 9.81 − 9.7803 = 0.0297 m/s² (it thinks it is sinking).
  2. Speed: v = a·t = 0.0297 × 10 = 0.297 m/s.
  3. Height: Δz = ½·a·t² = ½ × 0.0297 × 10² = 1.485 m, and it grows with t².
  4. So an estimator needs the right g, while a weight by hand does not. PX4's EKF2 [40] uses version 3 [29], and its GPS and barometer correct what is left.

Show solution

2.1.8 Your quadrotor or pentarotor: CL becomes the thrust coefficient CT

A multirotor has no wing, so a wing's CL does not fit it. Its "lift" is the thrust of its rotors. Each blade is a small spinning wing, but nobody adds up the blades one by one: engineers measure the whole propeller and pack it into one number, the thrust coefficient CT (definition of the UIUC Propeller Data Site [25]).

Trotor = CT · ρ · n² · d⁴

Show solution

TRY IT 16 · QUADROTOR HOVER SPEED
A 0.80 kg quadrotor has four of these propellers (CT = 0.0962). At what rpm does it hover at sea level?
Show solution
  1. Hover: N · CT · ρ · n² · d⁴ = m·g, so n = √( m·g / (N · CT · ρ · d⁴) ).
  2. W = m·g = 0.80 × 9.8062 = 7.845 N.
  3. N · CT · ρ · d⁴ = 4 × 0.0962 × 1.225 × 0.00026014 = 0.00012263.
  4. n = √(7.845 / 0.00012263) = 252.9 rev/s, and rpm = 60·n: about 15 200 rpm.
TRY IT 17 · PENTAROTOR
A pentarotor (five of the same propellers) has mass 1.0 kg. At what rpm does it hover? What do you notice when you compare it with Try it 16?
Show solution
  1. Load per rotor: Trotor = m·g / N = 1.0 × 9.8062 / 5 = 1.961 N.
  2. The quadrotor's load per rotor: m·g / N = 7.845 / 4 = 1.961 N, the same.
  3. One rotor alone: Trotor = CT·ρ·n²·d⁴ gives n = √( Trotor / (CT·ρ·d⁴) ): no N and no m left in it.
  4. Same load, same propeller, same air: the same speed, 252.9 rev/s, about 15 200 rpm. What sets the rotor speed is the load per rotor, not the number of rotors.
TRY IT 18 · HIGH FIELD
The quadrotor of Try it 16 flies in Denver (ρ = 1.049 kg/m³, Try it 8). At what speed must its rotors spin to hover?
Show solution

  2. Denver's g is 9.7965 m/s² (Try it 3b): g2 / g1 = 9.7965 / 9.8062 = 0.9990.

TRY IT 18a · THRUST IN FAST FLIGHT
The propeller of Try it 15 has CT0 = 0.0962 and (made-up practice numbers) CT1 = −0.06, CT2 = −0.05. The drone flies at V = 15 m/s with its rotors still at the hover speed n = 252.9 rev/s. Find J, CT(J) and the thrust of one rotor, and compare with the hover thrust 1.961 N.
Show solution

TRY IT 18b · THE MOTOR DELAY
Take a simulator step of Δt = 0.004 s (250 Hz, the rate of the x500's IMU). An x500 rotor turns at 500 rad/s when the command jumps to 800 rad/s. Find its speed after one step, and the time to cover 63 % of the jump.
Show solution
