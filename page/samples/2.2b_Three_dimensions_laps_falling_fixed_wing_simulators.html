2.2.7 Three dimensions: the same ideas, three numbers

A real drone moves in space, not on a line. The step from one dimension to three is small: use three numbers instead of one, one for each of three directions at right angles to each other. In this course x points east, y points north and z points up, so up is + as on page 2.1b. The origin O is a point on the ground that you choose, for example the launch pad. This choice of axes is called a world frame: a set of axes fixed to the ground, not to the drone. (Different tools choose different axes; PX4 [29] keeps z pointing down, which is called NED. The frames NED and ENU, and how to change from one to the other, come in a later section. This page uses x east, y north, z up everywhere.)

The position vector p = (x, y, z) lists the three positions in meters. The velocity vector v = (vx, vy, vz) lists the three velocities in m/s, and the acceleration vector a = (ax, ay, az) lists the three accelerations in m/s². The speed is the length of the velocity vector, found with Pythagoras: |v| = √(vx² + vy² + vz²). The speed is never negative.

The key fact: each direction is its own straight-line problem. The x motion does not care about the y motion. If the acceleration vector is constant, the four equations of page 2.2a hold separately for x, for y and for z. For example p = p0 + v0·t + ½·a·t² means three equations, one for each component. (Dellaert & Hutchinson, Introduction to Robotics and Perception [2, §7.2.2.1], write the same link between position and velocity as a vector equation in their navigation frame, which is a world frame with the axes east, north, up in their example: roboticsbook.org/S72_drone_actions.html. Ahmed, Drone physics [26], also describes position and velocity as vectors in an inertial frame. The acceleration is defined as the rate of change of the velocity by NASA Glenn, Flight Equations with Drag [21], and by MIT 16.00, Theory of Flight [24]; see page 2.2a.)

The example: a drone starts 30 m above the launch pad at p0 = (0, 0, 30) m. Its velocity is v0 = (12, 5, −3) m/s: it flies east and north and sinks slowly. Its acceleration is constant: a = (1.0, 0.5, −0.5) m/s². We look at t = 4.0 s.

THINK 5 · TWO LIMITS, ONE GATE

Situation. A drone starts at the pad, p₀ = (0, 0, 0). A gate hangs 90 m east, 60 m north and 20 m above the pad. The drone flies a straight line at a constant velocity; the short ramps at the start and the end are ignored. Two limits apply. The numbers are made up for practice. The horizontal speed may be at most 10.0 m/s, and the climb rate may be at most 3.0 m/s. Figure 2 shows the gate from above, from the side, and the time that each limit needs.

Questions. (a) What is the shortest time to the gate? (b) What are the three velocity components then? (c) What is the speed? (d) What changes if the gate is 40 m above the pad?

Think first.

  1. Each direction is its own straight-line problem (2.2.7). What is the shortest time that each limit allows on its own?
  2. The drone must satisfy both limits with one flight. Do the two times add up, or does one of them decide?
  3. If one limit decides, does the drone use the other limit completely?
HintAll three motions start and end together. Find the time for the horizontal part and the time for the climb separately, then compare.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: the horizontal limit is about the horizontal distance, which is the diagonal of the east and north parts. √(90² + 60²) = √11700 = 108.17 m.
  2. Why this step: each limit gives the shortest time of its own part. t = 108.17 / 10.0 = 10.82 s for the horizontal part and t = 20 / 3.0 = 6.67 s for the climb.
  3. Why this step: the horizontal part and the climb happen at the same time, so the drone needs the longer of the two times. T = max(10.82, 6.67) = 10.82 s. Adding the times, 10.82 + 6.67 = 17.49 s, would be a mistake.
  4. Why this step: with T known, each component is its distance divided by T. East: 90 / 10.82 = 8.32 m/s. North: 60 / 10.82 = 5.55 m/s. Up: 20 / 10.82 = 1.85 m/s.
  5. Why this step: the speed is the length of the velocity vector (2.2.7). √(8.32² + 5.55² + 1.85²) = √(69.22 + 30.80 + 3.42) = √103.44 = 10.17 m/s.

Check your answer. The horizontal speed alone must equal the horizontal limit: √(8.32² + 5.55²) = √(69.22 + 30.80) = √100.02 = 10.00 m/s. The climb rate 1.85 m/s is below the limit of 3.0 m/s, so the climb limit is not used completely. The total speed, 10.17 m/s, is above 10.0 m/s. That is allowed because the first limit is about the horizontal speed only.

What if … the gate were 40 m above the pad?

Show the answerThe climb needs t = 40 / 3.0 = 13.33 s, which is longer than 10.82 s, so now the climb decides. The horizontal speed falls to 108.17 / 13.33 = 8.11 m/s, below its limit. The speed is √(8.11² + 3.0²) = √(65.77 + 9.00) = √74.77 = 8.65 m/s. A steeper gate makes the drone slower overall.

2.2.8 A lap as a list of phases

A lap is a list of phases. In each phase the acceleration is constant, so the four equations of page 2.2a work, and the end values of one phase are the start values of the next. This is how a planner and a simulator build a whole lap.

This lap is idealised, not measured. The saved simulator runs of this course contain no log with time and position, so the numbers below are made up to show the method. A real flight log would give the same three graphs, with rounder corners.

The drone flies along the track. Let s be the distance along the track measured from the start line, like the odometer of a car; the speed v = ds/dt is the rate at which the odometer changes, and a = dv/dt is the acceleration along the track. In the corner the direction of the velocity changes too; that is an acceleration at a right angle to the track, and it is not drawn here (the turn comes in a later section).

How to read this table. The table has five rows, one for each phase, and a last row for the whole lap. Read a row from left to right: the phase, its acceleration, the velocity at the start and the end of it, its time, the distance it covers, and the distance along the track at its end. It matters for flying because every lap time is a sum of phases like these. Here is every symbol. a is the acceleration along the track in m/s²; + speeds the drone up, − slows it down. v₀ → v are the speeds at the start and at the end of the phase, in m/s. t is the time of the phase in s: for a phase with a ≠ 0 it is t = (v − v₀) / a, for a cruise it is given. Δs is the distance covered in the phase, in m, from equation 4 of page 2.2a, Δs = ½·(v₀ + v)·t. s at the end is the sum of the Δs so far. ttotal and stotal are the sums over the lap, and vavg = stotal / ttotal is the average speed of the lap in m/s.   2. Why this step: the lap is still 210 m, so the braking now frees some distance. 42.0 − 28.0 = 14.0 m.
  3. Why this step: the freed distance is flown by the cruise phase, which starts earlier and ends later. At the cruise speed the extra time is 14.0 / 20 = 0.7 s. The cruise lasts 3.0 + 0.7 = 3.7 s.

What if … the team improves the sprint (phase 5) from +6.0 to +8.0 m/s² instead?

Show the answerThe sprint takes t = (20 − 8) / 8.0 = 1.5 s and covers Δs = ½ × (8 + 20) × 1.5 = 21.0 m, so it frees 28.0 − 21.0 = 7.0 m. Those meters cost 7.0 / 20 = 0.35 s of cruise. The gain is (2.0 − 1.5) − 0.35 = 0.15 s, and the lap takes 17.0 − 0.15 = 16.85 s. Improving a phase by 0.5 s gains only 0.15 s.

2.2.9 Free fall: the motors stop

If the motors of a drone stop, the only thing left that acts on it is its weight (the drag of the air is added later in this section). The drone then has the acceleration a = −g in our frame where up is +. This is true for every mass; section 2.3 shows why. We use g of the place, version 4 of 2.1.2.

Start from rest at the height h above the ground: z₀ = h, v₀ = 0, a = −g. Equation 2 of page 2.2a becomes z = h − ½·g·t² and equation 1 becomes v = −g·t (the minus sign means down). The drone reaches the ground when z = 0, which gives the fall time tfall = √(2·h / g). The impact speed is the size of the velocity then, |v| = g·tfall, which equals √(2·g·h) (equation 3 gives the same without the time).

How to read this table. The table has four rows, one for each starting height, all at the home field, g = 9.8062 m/s². Read a row from left to right: the height, the fall time with the number under the root, and the impact speed. It matters for flying because it tells how long you have, and how fast the drone hits, after a motor failure. Here is every symbol. h is the starting height above the ground, in m; a bigger h gives a longer fall. g is the gravity number, in m/s². tfall is the fall time in s. 2·h / g is the number under the root, in s². vimpact = g·tfall is the speed at the ground, in m/s; the fall time grows with the square root of h, and so does the impact speed. 2.2.10 Fixed-wing aircraft: takeoff and landing rolls

Everything above holds for a fixed-wing aircraft too. One thing is different, and it changes how the aircraft moves: a fixed-wing aircraft cannot stop in the air and cannot hover. Its lift needs the air to stream past the wing: L = ½·ρ·V²·S·CL (page 2.1a, section 2.1.7), and at V = 0 the lift is 0. So the aircraft must always keep a forward speed above a lowest speed at which the wing still carries the weight. That lowest speed is called the stall speed; why it exists is the subject of a later section on angle of attack and stall, and here it is only a name. The consequence for motion: a fixed-wing aircraft starts from rest on the ground, must accelerate along a runway until it reaches the takeoff speed, and after landing must brake along a runway to rest. A multirotor lifts straight up and has no runway.

Both rolls are the same problem as the braking distance of page 2.2a, equation 3 with v₀ = 0 (takeoff) or v = 0 (landing): s = V² / (2·|a|) and t = V / |a|. Here V is the speed at the end of the takeoff roll or at the start of the landing roll, in m/s. |a| is the size of the constant acceleration along the runway, in m/s²; s is the roll length in m and t the roll time in s. A longer runway is needed for a higher V (the square!) or a smaller |a|. 2.2.11 From formula to simulator: how a computer turns a into v and v into x

The formulas of page 2.2a give x at any time t in one step, but only when a is constant. A simulator, an autopilot and a planner do not work this way. They move forward in small time steps. At each step they know the state now (the position and the velocity) and the acceleration now, and they compute the state a short time later. We use these letters: Δt is the length of one time step, in seconds; a smaller Δt gives a smaller error. k is the number of the step: 0, 1, 2 and so on. xk, vk and ak are the position (m), the velocity (m/s) and the acceleration (m/s²) at the time tk = k·Δt. A rule for the next step has the form "new v from the old v and a" and "new x from the old x and v".

Where a comes from

In Project AirSim [30] (its "Fast Physics" documentation [42]) the acceleration is the sum of the forces divided by the mass, plus gravity. This is section 2.1 (the forces) and section 2.3 (the division by m); here we only look at the result. The line is copied from the reference page (row PA-8-01):

const Wrench total_wrench = external_wrench + drag_wrench + lift_drag_wrench;

kin_no_collision.accels.linear = total_wrench.force * mass_inv + env_gravity;

Project AirSim, physics/src/fast_physics.cpp:521-525 @ c80cff5 [30]. In the reference data the formula is written a = (Frotors + Fext + Fdrag + Fliftdrag) / m + gvec with gvec = (0, 0, +g). Gravity is +g along z, so in this frame z points down. That is the frame difference of section 2.2.7 again.

The most complete rule in the code: Project AirSim

Of the code bases in the reference data, Project AirSim [30] has the most detailed rule: it uses the acceleration at both ends of the step. Two blocks of code advance the state by one step of dt_sec. The first block gives the new velocity from the old acceleration and the new acceleration:

kin_no_collision.twist.linear =
    cur_kin.twist.linear +
    (cur_kin.accels.linear + kin_no_collision.accels.linear) * 0.5f * dt_sec;

Project AirSim, physics/src/fast_physics.cpp:541-543 @ c80cff5 [30] (reference row PA-10-03). In letters: vk+1 = vk + ½·(ak + ak+1)·Δt. The reference data calls this the trapezoidal rule and says it is second-order accurate.

The second block gives the new position from the old velocity and the old acceleration:

const Vector3 ave_vel_lin =
    cur_kin.twist.linear + cur_kin.accels.linear * (0.5f * dt_sec);
const Vector3 ave_vel_ang =
    cur_kin.twist.angular + cur_kin.accels.angular * (0.5f * dt_sec);
kin_no_collision.pose.position = cur_kin.pose.position + ave_vel_lin * dt_sec;

Project AirSim, physics/src/fast_physics.cpp:495-499, 570 @ c80cff5 [30] (reference row PA-10-02). In letters: vavg = vk + ½·ak·Δt and xk+1 = xk + vavg·Δt. (The lines about angular do the same for the rotation; this page follows the linear lines only.)

Why this rule is exact when a is constant. With a constant a, the velocity changes in a straight line, and the average velocity over one step is the velocity in the middle of the step, vk + ½·a·Δt. So xk+1 = xk + vk·Δt + ½·a·Δt², which is equation 2 of page 2.2a for one step. The velocity rule is equation 1: with ak = ak+1 = a it gives vk+1 = vk + a·Δt. The reference data adds two facts: the position step uses the previous acceleration only (a code comment asks whether the new velocity should be used instead), and the force at step k+1 is computed with the half-step velocity, so the rule is explicit (no equation has to be solved).

The order inside one tick. In the same code base the clock calls, in this order: the physics step, the environment, the sensors, the controller, the actuators, and then the new wrench (force and torque) is set for the next step (core_sim/src/scene.cpp:1068-1141 @ c80cff5 [30], reference row PA-10-01). So a command given after the sensors acts for the next Δt: the controller always works with one step of delay. The step is 3 ms in the examples of the documentation and 20 ms if the configuration leaves it out (docs/config_scene.md:68-69, 79 [43]).

The simpler rules of PX4, Gazebo and the textbook

PX4's simulator SIH [39], the PX4 estimator EKF2 [40] and Gazebo's physics engine DART [34] use simpler rules. First, PX4 SIH (reference row g10-sih-integration):

The most complete form: PX4's estimator on a turning, round Earth

All the rules above treat the ground as a flat floor that stands still. The velocity step of the same estimator function is more complete. It is in the function Ekf::predictState, ekf.cpp lines 233-276 @ d28a642 [29]. EKF2 [40] starts from the change of velocity that the accelerometers measured, and it adds three corrections: gravity, the Coriolis acceleration and a transport-rate acceleration. The Coriolis acceleration appears because the Earth turns. The transport-rate acceleration appears because the drone moves over a curved surface, so the north, east and down axes turn while the drone flies. The derivation script of the estimator names a report by Solà, Quaternion kinematics for the error-state Kalman filter [54], as its basis (derivation.py:33-35 @ d28a642 [29]). This subsection reads the code in three steps.

Step 1: the measured change of velocity. The first lines turn the accelerometer data into a change of velocity in the earth axes (north, east, down) [29, ekf.cpp:259-261]:

    const Vector3f delta_vel_bias_scaled = getAccelBias() * imu_delayed.delta_vel_dt;
    const Vector3f corrected_delta_vel = imu_delayed.delta_vel - delta_vel_bias_scaled;
    const Vector3f corrected_delta_vel_ef = _R_to_earth * corrected_delta_vel;

Here is every letter. Δvimu is imu_delayed.delta_vel, the change of velocity that the accelerometers measured during one step, in m/s, along the axes of the drone body. An accelerometer measures the force per unit of mass without gravity, so a drone that rests on a table reads one g upward. b is getAccelBias(), the accelerometer bias in m/s², which EKF2 estimates while it flies [40]. Δt is delta_vel_dt, the length of the step in s, so b·Δt is delta_vel_bias_scaled. R is _R_to_earth, the rotation matrix from the body axes to the earth axes. The result corrected_delta_vel_ef (the letters ef mean earth frame) is R·(Δvimu − b·Δt).

Step 2: gravity, Coriolis and transport. The velocity update is in these lines. The old velocity was saved before as vel_last (line 264), and line 267 added corrected_delta_vel_ef [29, ekf.cpp:270-273]:

    const Vector3f gravity_acceleration(0.f, 0.f, _gravity);
    const Vector3f coriolis_acceleration = -2.f * _earth_rate_NED.cross(vel_last);
    const Vector3f transport_rate_acceleration = -transport_rate.cross(vel_last);
    _state.vel += (gravity_acceleration + coriolis_acceleration + transport_rate_acceleration) * imu_delayed.delta_vel_dt;

Here is every letter. v is the velocity in north, east, down axes (_state.vel), and vel_last is its value before the step. g is the vector (0, 0, _gravity). The third axis points down, so gravity has only a third part, and the size _gravity is set at the latitude of the drone by LatLonAlt::Wgs84::gravity (ekf.cpp:240). It is added because the accelerometers do not feel gravity: a still drone reads −g in the third axis, and the added +g·Δt cancels it. Ω is the spin rate of the Earth, CONSTANTS_EARTH_SPIN_RATE = 7.292115 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s (src/lib/geo/geo.h:57 @ d28a642 [29]), the value that 2.1.2 of page 2.1a takes from WGS84 [45]. ΩNED is _earth_rate_NED, the spin vector of the Earth written in north, east, down axes. ρ is transport_rate, the rate at which the north, east, down axes turn because the drone moves over the curved Earth. The symbol × is the cross product of two vectors. Its result is a vector at right angles to both, and its length is the product of the two lengths times the sine of the angle between them.

Step 3: where the two rates come from. The line that sets the spin vector is ekf.cpp:239 [29]:

The function calcEarthRateNED is in src/modules/ekf2/EKF/ekf_helper.cpp:59-64 [29]. φ is the latitude in radians (lat_rad):

Vector3f Ekf::calcEarthRateNED(float lat_rad) const
{
    return Vector3f(CONSTANTS_EARTH_SPIN_RATE * cosf(lat_rad),
            0.0f,
            -CONSTANTS_EARTH_SPIN_RATE * sinf(lat_rad));
}

In letters, ΩNED = (Ω·cos φ, 0, −Ω·sin φ): the Earth spins about an axis that points north and up at the latitude φ. The line that sets the transport rate is ekf.cpp:249 [29]:

const Vector3f transport_rate = _gpos.computeAngularRateNavFrame(_state.vel);

Vector3f LatLonAlt::computeAngularRateNavFrame(const Vector3f &v_ned) const
{
    double r_n;
    double r_e;
    computeRadiiOfCurvature(_latitude_rad, r_n, r_e);
    return Vector3f(
               v_ned(1) / (static_cast<float>(r_e) + _altitude),
               -v_ned(0) / (static_cast<float>(r_n) + _altitude),
               -v_ned(1) * tanf(_latitude_rad) / (static_cast<float>(r_e) + _altitude));
}

In letters, ρ = ( vE / (RE + h), −vN / (RN + h), −vE·tan φ / (RE + h) ). Here vN and vE are the north and east parts of the velocity (v_ned(0) and v_ned(1)), h is the altitude (_altitude), and RN and RE are the radii of curvature of the Earth surface in the north and east directions (r_n and r_e, computed by computeRadiiOfCurvature, which this page does not read). On a ball, both radii would be the radius of the ball.

The full update, in letters, as the code writes it. Line 267 adds the measured change, and line 273 adds the three corrections multiplied by Δt. Both lines use the velocity from before the step:

v ← v + R·(Δvimu − b·Δt) + ( g − 2·ΩNED × v − ρ × v )·Δt

The last bracket is the acceleration that must be added to what the accelerometers feel. If the Earth did not turn (Ω = 0) and the ground were flat (ρ = 0), the bracket would shrink to g, and the sum of the measured part and g·Δt would be a·Δt, the velocity rule of rows 2 to 4 of the table. The position step that follows is the trapezoid of line 276.

How large are the two terms? The Coriolis acceleration has the size 2·Ω·|v|·sin(angle), so its largest possible value, for a velocity at a right angle to the spin axis, is 2·Ω·|v|. For a drone at 20 m/s: 2 × Ω × v = 2 × 7.292115 × 10⁻⁵ × 20 = 2.917 × 10⁻³ m/s², about 0.0003 times g. Without any correction, this acceleration moves the estimated position by ½·a·t². During the lap of 2.2.8 (17 s): ½ × 0.002917 × 17² = ½ × 0.002917 × 289 = 0.42 m. During 5 minutes (300 s): ½ × 0.002917 × 300² = ½ × 0.002917 × 90000 = 131 m. (The real error is smaller at most places, because only the part of the velocity at a right angle to the spin axis counts, and it also depends on the direction of flight; the numbers are the upper bound.) The transport-rate term is about v² / R, where R is the radius of the Earth. With the equator radius of 6 378 137 m from 2.1.2 [45]: v² / R = 20² / 6 378 137 = 6.271 × 10⁻⁵ m/s². Over 17 s it moves the position by ½ × 0.00006271 × 289 = 0.0091 m, and over 300 s by ½ × 0.00006271 × 90000 = 2.8 m. It is about 47 times smaller than the Coriolis term.

Why the other rules leave it out. The reference data has no Coriolis or transport-rate row for Project AirSim [30] or for Gazebo [31], and the acceleration line of Project AirSim (fast_physics.cpp:525, shown above) contains only the force, the mass and gravity. The flat-Earth rules of page 2.2a have no such term either. The reference data records formulas and files; it gives no reason for this choice, and this page does not invent one. What the sizes show is this: in a lap of 17 s the largest Coriolis error is 0.42 m, and the transport-rate error is below 0.01 m. These are small beside the error of 1.00 m that a coarse time step gives in the table above. After 5 minutes the Coriolis error is 131 m. A rule that runs for seconds can leave the terms out. An estimator that runs for minutes without a position fix may have to include them (Think 10); the next part shows two papers that write the same terms, and one measured case in which the terms made no difference.

Two research papers read next to the code

Hager & Bryne [60, §2.2, eq. 4-7] write the velocity equation in north, east, down axes on a turning Earth as v̇ = R·f − (2·ΩNED + ρ) × v + g. Here f is the bias-corrected accelerometer reading, which they call the specific force (page 2.1a, 2.1.2), and ΩNED and ρ are the Earth rate and the transport rate of this section (they write ωie and ωen). Their attitude equation subtracts Rᵀ·(ΩNED + ρ) from the gyroscope rate. Set next to the code, line by line, the two agree. Line 273 adds −2·Ω × v − ρ × v = −(2·Ω + ρ) × v, and line 250 subtracts Rᵀ·(Ω + ρ)·Δt from the gyro angle. The terms, the signs and the axes are the same: north, east, down, turning with the drone. The paper adds a comparison. It derives four versions of the error-state filter: one with additive errors in north, east, down axes (the classical form), two with invariant errors on a group called SE₂(3) in Earth-centred axes (left and right), and one with additive errors in Earth-centred axes [60, abstract, §1, §7]. It states that in the classical form the error dynamics depend on the trajectory, and that the invariant forms can make them independent of it for a class of systems called group-affine [60, §1]. The derivation script of EKF2 writes the errors of position and velocity as sums and the rotation error as a small rotation on the left (derivation.py:151-153 @ d28a642 [29]), which is the form that the paper calls the standard one [60, §3.1].

Vial et al. [58, §III, eq. 1, p. 1349; §VI-C, eq. 39, p. 1355] keep the books differently. Their world frame is one north, east, down frame fixed at a reference latitude, so it does not move with the vehicle. Their velocity equation is v̇ = R·a + g − 2·[Ω]× v − [Ω]×²·p: a Coriolis term and a centrifugal term, and no transport rate. Their paper also preintegrates the IMU data and the data of a linear-velocity sensor together on a group called SEN(3), and keeps the Earth-rotation compensation as a separate factor [58, abstract, §IV-B, eq. 12]. So the three agree on the Coriolis term and differ in how they write the rest.

Row 10 of the autopilot map: what an error-state filter is

The estimator of PX4 (row 10 of the autopilot map) is an error-state filter [40]. The PX4 guide gives the reason in one sentence: To improve stability, an "error-state" formulation is implemented (tuning_the_ecl_ekf.md:23-24 @ d28a642 [29]). Solà [54, §5.2, p. 53] describes the idea in five steps. Figure 14 draws them as a loop.

  1. Split. The true state is the nominal state combined with a small error state: xtrue = x ⊕ δx. For the position and the velocity the combination is a plain sum, ptrue = p + δp and vtrue = v + δv. For the orientation it is a product of quaternions, qtrue = q ⊗ δq [54, Table 3, p. 54].
  2. Integrate. The IMU data go into the nominal state at the high rate, and the noise is left out. So the nominal state slowly drifts [54, §5.2, p. 53]. The velocity step of 2.2.11 is this step.
  3. Predict the error. While only the IMU arrives, the filter predicts the error only. Its mean stays 0 and its covariance P grows (Think 10b) [54, eq. 268-269, p. 61-62].
  4. Correct. When other information arrives, such as a GPS fix or a camera, at a much lower rate than the IMU, the filter estimates the error from it [54, §5.2, eq. 274-278, p. 62-63].
  5. Inject and reset. The estimated error is added to the nominal state, the error is set back to 0, and the covariance is updated to match the reset [54, eq. 282-286, p. 64-65].

Why use it [54, §5.1, p. 52-53]. Solà gives four reasons. First, the orientation error has the fewest numbers possible: 3 numbers for 3 degrees of freedom. Second, the error stays near the origin, far from singularities such as gimbal lock, so the linearisation stays valid. Third, the error is small, so products of two errors can be dropped and the Jacobians are simple. Fourth, the error changes slowly, because the large motion is in the nominal state, so the corrections may come at a lower rate than the predictions.

What PX4's script does. The derivation script of EKF2 begins: Derivation of an error-state EKF based on Sola, Joan. "Quaternion kinematics for the error-state Kalman filter." It writes the true position and velocity as the nominal value plus the error, and the true quaternion as a small-angle rotation sf.Rot3(sf.Quaternion(xyz=(state_error["theta"] / 2), w=1)) multiplied on the left of the nominal quaternion (derivation.py:34-35, 151-155 @ d28a642 [29]). That is the global form of the angular error. Solà's main text uses the local form, and he gives the global form in his section 7, with the remark that evidence shows a global angular error "has better properties" [54, §5.3, p. 53; §7, p. 66-71]. This page read the script, not the C++ code that applies the correction.

Where the rest comes. The Jacobians Fx and H, the reset matrix G and the covariance P are not worked out here. They come in chapter 12 of Part III, plan row 12.1 (The Kalman filter) and row 12.2 (Camera + IMU together). Think 10b does the loop once with one number.

Where research is going: position, velocity and time stepped together

On this page the rotation, the velocity and the position are separate things, each with its own rule. Pérez-Ruiz & Solà [59, §I] write that most LiDAR-inertial frameworks hold the state in the same way, as a compound (or bundle) state on separate parts, usually the rotation group and six plain numbers, SO(3) × R⁶. They write that this decomposition "neglects certain kinematic couplings between translational and rotational motion" [59, §I]. Their filter, LIMOncello, keeps the state on another group, SGal(3), "a 10-dimensional Lie group that can be interpreted as the group of transformations that move points in both space and time" [59, §II-C]. One element is a 5×5 matrix with the rows (R, v, p), (0, 1, t) and (0, 0, 1) [59, eq. 10]: R is the rotation, v the velocity, p the position and t the time. One IMU step is one line, Γ ← Γ ⊕ (0, a, ω, 1)ᵀ·Δt [59, eq. 12], "assuming inputs are constant during one sampling period". Here a and ω are, in the paper's words, "the angular rates and linear accelerations" [59, §II-C], and Δt is the time step. The accelerometer reading is not yet that acceleration: the paper models the reading as a = a⋆ + ba + na − Rᵀ·g [59, eq. 14b], with the true acceleration a⋆, a bias, noise and gravity in it, as on page 2.1a (2.1.2). The paper adds that on the older group SE₂(3) the same step is "often expressed as Ξ ← Ξ ⊕ Φ(Ξ) Δt, with Φ an auxiliary mapping", which makes it "less compact" [59, footnote 2].

  3. For (d), the answer is a comparison, not a single number. What do you compare?
HintUse the largest value 2·Ω·|v| of the Coriolis acceleration as the constant a. Then (a) is the formula ½·a·t² = 1.5 solved for t.

  2. Why this step: solve ½·a·t² = E for the time. t = √(2·E / a) = √(2 × 1.5 / 0.002917) = √1028.5 = 32.07 s.
  3. Why this step: the distance is the speed times that time. 20 × 32.07 = 641 m.
  4. Why this step: for (b), an accelerometer bias b is also a constant acceleration, so the bias with the same effect is b = 0.002917 m/s². THINK 10b ·THE ERROR STATE: ZERO MEAN, GROWING COVARIANCE, THEN ONE GPS FIX

Situation. Take the loop of Figure 14 for one number only: the velocity error along one axis (all other states are left out). The nominal velocity is integrated from the IMU every Δt = 0.01 s. The accelerometer noise has the standard deviation σ = 0.5 m/s² per reading (made up). Solà [54, eq. 262, p. 60] gives the variance that one step adds to the velocity error: Vi = σ²·Δt². The error starts with variance 0 at t = 0. At t = 10 s a GPS velocity of 12.10 m/s arrives, with the standard deviation 0.10 m/s (made up), while the nominal velocity says 12.30 m/s. Figure 19 draws the standard deviation of the velocity error.

Questions. (a) What is the mean of the error state during the first 10 s? (b) What are the variance and the standard deviation of the velocity error after 1 s and after 10 s? (c) At the GPS fix: what are the gain K, the estimated error, the new nominal velocity and the new variance? (d) What do the nominal state and the error state hold after the reset?

Think first.

  1. The prediction step is δx̂ ← Fx·δx̂ and P ← Fx·P·Fxᵀ + Fi·Qi·Fiᵀ [54, eq. 268-269, p. 61]. For one velocity number Fx = 1. What happens to a mean that starts at 0, and what does each step add to P?
  2. The steps are independent, so their variances add. How many steps are there in 1 s and in 10 s?
  3. For (c), the correction is K = P·Hᵀ·(H·P·Hᵀ + V)⁻¹ with H = 1, and then δx̂ ← K·(y − h) and P ← (I − K·H)·P [54, eq. 274-278, p. 62-63]. Which number is y − h?
HintA mean that is multiplied by 1 and has nothing added stays 0. The variance adds Vi in every step. The measurement variance is V = 0.10² and y − h is the GPS value minus the nominal value.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: the mean is multiplied by Fx = 1 in every step and nothing is added to it, so the mean of the error state stays 0 while only the IMU arrives. The estimate does not move, but the uncertainty does.
  2. Why this step: the variance that one step adds. Vi = σ²·Δt² = 0.5² × 0.01² = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ (m/s)².
  3. Why this step: for (b), the variances of independent steps add. After 1 s there are 100 steps: 100 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ = 2.5 × 10⁻³ (m/s)², so the standard deviation is √(2.5 × 10⁻³) = 0.0500 m/s. After 10 s there are 1000 steps: 1000 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ = 2.5 × 10⁻² (m/s)², and √(2.5 × 10⁻²) = 0.1581 m/s.
  4. Why this step: the gain says how much of the surprise to believe. With P = 0.025 and V = 0.10² = 0.010: K = P / (P + V) = 0.025 / (0.025 + 0.010) = 0.7143. The surprise is the GPS value minus the nominal value, 12.10 − 12.30 = −0.20 m/s.
  5. Why this step: the estimated error is the gain times the surprise. δv̂ = K × (12.10 − 12.30) = 0.7143 × (−0.20) = −0.1429 m/s. Inject it into the nominal state: v ← 12.30 + (−0.1429) = 12.157 m/s.
  6. Why this step: reset the error to 0 and update the variance for the correction. P ← (1 − K)·P = (1 − 0.7143) × 0.025 = 7.14 × 10⁻³ (m/s)², so √(7.14 × 10⁻³) = 0.0845 m/s. For (d): the nominal state holds 12.157 m/s, the error state holds 0 with the variance 7.14 × 10⁻³, and the loop of Figure 14 starts again.

Check your answer. Ten times the time gives √10 times the standard deviation: √(1000 / 100) = 3.162, and 0.1581 / 0.0500 = 3.16. The new variance must be smaller than both the old one (0.025) and the GPS one (0.010). The shortcut for two independent estimates gives the same value: 1 / (1/0.025 + 1/0.010) = 1 / (40 + 100) = 7.14 × 10⁻³.

What if … the GPS were worse, with the standard deviation 0.50 m/s?

Show the answerThen V = 0.25 and K = 0.025 / (0.025 + 0.25) = 0.0909: the filter believes the GPS little. The estimated error is 0.0909 × (−0.20) = −0.0182 m/s, the nominal velocity becomes 12.30 + (−0.0182) = 12.282 m/s, and the variance falls only to (1 − 0.0909) × 0.025 = 2.27 × 10⁻² (m/s)².
THINK 10c ·ONE SGAL(3) STEP, DONE BY HAND

Situation. A drone starts at rest at x = 0, at the time t = 0, with the rotation R = I (its body axes are the world axes). Its acceleration is the constant a = 2 m/s² along x: the motion of the tables of 2.2.11. This is the true acceleration, after the bias and gravity have been taken out of the accelerometer reading (the box above, eq. 14b). The time step is Δt = 0.5 s and the drone does not turn (ω = 0). In SGal(3) [59, §II-C, eq. 10-12] the state is the 5×5 matrix Γ with the rows (R, v, p), (0, 1, t), (0, 0, 1). One step multiplies Γ by the matrix of the step, which is the exponential of the matrix of the step vector. The step vector is (0, a, ω, 1)·Δt, and its matrix has the rows ([ω]×, a, 0), (0, 0, 1) and (0, 0, 0), all multiplied by Δt [59, eq. 11]. Figure 20 draws Γ after two steps and the position after every step.

Questions. (a) With ω = 0, what are the blocks of the step matrix? (b) Multiply Γ by the step matrix twice, starting from rest: what are v, p and t after the second step? (c) Compare with the exact motion and with the tables of 2.2.11. (d) Which rule of the table in 2.2.11 is this step?

Think first.

  1. With ω = 0 the rotation block of the matrix of the step vector is 0. Which two entries are left?
  2. Multiply that matrix by itself, block by block. Which block of the product is not 0? What is the third power?
  3. When two matrices of the form (R, v, p; 0, 1, t; 0, 0, 1) are multiplied, which block of the product holds the position?
HintThe exponential of a matrix N is I + N + ½·N² + … . If N³ = 0 the sum stops after the N² term. In the product of Γ = (R, v, p; 0, 1, t; 0, 0, 1) and S = (ΔR, Δv, Δp; 0, 1, Δt; 0, 0, 1) the position block is R·Δp + v·Δt + p.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: with ω = 0 the matrix N of the step vector keeps two entries, a·Δt in the velocity column and Δt in the time column. Its square has one block that is not 0, the product of the two, a·Δt × Δt = a·Δt², in the position column, and its cube is 0. So the sum stops, and the step matrix S has the blocks ΔR = I, Δv = a·Δt = 2 × 0.5 = 1.0 m/s, Δp = ½·a·Δt² = ½ × 2 × 0.5² = 0.25 m and Δt = 0.5 s.
  2. Why this step: multiply Γ and S block by block. The result has the blocks R·ΔR, v + R·Δv, p + v·Δt + R·Δp and t + Δt. So the position takes the old velocity times the time step, and the time block adds up the steps. Step 1, from rest with R = I: v₁ = 0 + 1.0 = 1.0 m/s, p₁ = 0 + 0 × 0.5 + 0.25 = 0.25 m, t₁ = 0 + 0.5 = 0.5 s.
  3. Why this step: step 2 uses the numbers of step 1. v₂ = 1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0 m/s, p₂ = 0.25 + 1.0 × 0.5 + 0.25 = 1.00 m, t₂ = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0 s.
  4. Why this step: for (c), the exact motion at t = 1.0 s is x = ½·a·t² = ½ × 2 × 1.0² = 1.00 m and v = a·t = 2 × 1.0 = 2.0 m/s, the row t = 1.0 s of the table in 2.2.11 for rules 1 and 2. Two more steps give v₃ = 2.0 + 1.0 = 3.0 m/s, p₃ = 1.00 + 2.0 × 0.5 + 0.25 = 2.25 m and p₄ = 2.25 + 3.0 × 0.5 + 0.25 = 4.00 m, the other rows of that table.
  5. Why this step: for (d), with R = I the position block reads p + v·Δt + ½·a·Δt², which is xk+1 = xk + (vk + ½·a·Δt)·Δt, rule 1 of the table (Project AirSim [30]), and it is exact for a constant a, as the table says. The time block gives tk = k·Δt, as in 2.2.11.

Check your answer. One step of 1.0 s must give the same as two steps of 0.5 s: a·Δt = 2 × 1.0 = 2.0 m/s and ½·a·Δt² = ½ × 2 × 1.0² = 1.00 m, with the time 1.0 s. The result agrees with step 3 of the solution.

What if … the drone is turned, so that R turns the body x axis into the world y axis, and the increments are the same?

Show the answerThe increments are measured in the body axes, and the product turns them into the world axes before it adds them. From rest, v = R·Δv = (0, 1.0, 0) m/s and p = R·Δp = (0, 0.25, 0) m: the drone speeds up north. If the drone also turns during the step (ω ≠ 0), the block [ω]× of the step matrix is not 0. The square of the matrix then also has the block [ω]×·a·Δt² in the velocity column, and the sum no longer stops after two terms. The turn during the step enters the increments of velocity and position. This is the coupling of rotation and translation that Pérez-Ruiz & Solà say the compound state neglects [59, §I]. This page does not compute it, and the paper gives no drone numbers.

PROBLEM 1 · TWO GATES
Gate A is at x = 0 and gate B at x = 80 m on a straight line. A drone passes A at rest. It accelerates at +4.0 m/s² up to 16 m/s, cruises, and brakes at −4.0 m/s² so that it passes B at 12 m/s. Show solution

PROBLEM 2 · WIND ON THE GROUND SPEED

PROBLEM 4 · BRAKING BEFORE A GATE

Show solution
