2.2d Real scenarios: thinking problems with wind, payload and turbulence

Part I · Chapter 2 · section 2, page 4 of 4 · about 2 hours · paper and a calculator · pages before: 2.2a, 2.2b and 2.2c

Page 2.2a: 2.2.1-2.2.6, Think 1-4. Page 2.2b: 2.2.7-2.2.12, Think 5-10 and 10a-10c. Page 2.2c: 2.2.13-2.2.19, Think 11-14. This page: 2.2.20-2.2.25, Think 15-25. Think numbers run on across the four pages. Figures are numbered on each page from 1; a figure on another page is always named with its page and its subject.

Goal. After this page you can solve a flight problem in which several ideas meet: falling, laps, speed, acceleration, takeoff, height, braking and the time of a roll, with wind from any direction, a payload and turbulence. You can choose the idea that fits, watch for the trap, test whether a plan is possible, and work backward from a measurement. You also see why the parts of the autopilot of the autopilot map (the planner, the estimator and the simulator) need these ideas.
How this page works. Every problem is a Think problem, and it has the same five parts. (1) The situation, with a figure that draws it. (2) Think first: two to four questions about what is asked, what is unknown, which ideas apply and where a trap may be; a hint is hidden under them. (3) The solution: numbered steps, and every step starts with Why this step, one or two sentences of reasoning, before the formula in letters, then in numbers, then the result. (4) Check your answer: a test of units, of a limit case, or a second method. (5) What if …: a twist that changes one condition, with a short hidden answer. Try the problem on paper before you open the solution. Each problem names the sections where its ideas are taught. Gravity: the home field is φ = 45°, h = 0 m, g = 9.8062 m/s², and Denver is φ = 39.74°, h = 1 600 m, g = 9.7965 m/s² and ρ = 1.049 kg/m³ (version 4 of 2.1.2 on page 2.1a). Drag is ignored in every problem unless it says otherwise; where drag would change the answer, the problem says in words what it would change. Numbers that are made up for practice are marked "made up". Axes: x east, y north, z up, as on page 2.2b.

Two conventions that every problem uses

A bearing is a compass direction in degrees, measured clockwise from north: 0° is north, 90° is east, 180° is south and 270° is west. A wind is named after the direction it comes from. "Wind from 240°" comes from the south-west and blows toward 240° − 180° = 60°, which is north-east (page 2.1b, section 2.1.12 has the trap box). The wind arrow w therefore points toward 60°, and its east and north parts are (w·sin 60°, w·cos 60°).

Ground = air + wind. The drone moves through the air with the airspeed arrow, and the air itself moves over the ground with the wind arrow. The ground velocity is the sum of the two arrows (page 2.1b, section 2.1.12 writes it the other way round, V = vg − vw). Figure 1 draws the bearings on the left and the sum on the right, for a drone that flies north at 12 m/s in a wind of 6 m/s from 240°.

2.2.20 Falling after a motor failure, with a parcel and the wind

THINK 15 · THE MOTORS STOP IN A CROSSWIND, WITH A PARCEL

Situation. A delivery drone of 1.5 kg carries a parcel of 2.0 kg, so the total mass is 3.5 kg. It flies north at an airspeed of 12 m/s, 40 m above the ground. The wind comes from 240° at 6 m/s. At one moment all four motors stop. Drag is ignored (home field, g = 9.8062 m/s²). Ideas used: the ground velocity of 2.1.12 and the free fall of 2.2.9.

Questions. (a) What is the ground velocity at the moment of the failure? (b) How long does the fall last? (c) Where does the drone land, compared with the point below the failure? (d) How fast is it at impact?

Think first.

  1. Does the mass of the parcel change the fall time?
  2. At the moment the motors stop, which velocity does the drone have: the air velocity or the ground velocity?
  3. With drag ignored, what changes the horizontal velocity after the failure?
  4. The wind comes from 240°. Toward which bearing does it blow?
HintTurn "from 240°" into "toward 60°", split the wind into an east part and a north part, and add it to the airspeed arrow. After that, the horizontal and the vertical motion are two separate problems.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: the wind arrow must point where the air goes, not where it comes from. The air blows toward 240° − 180° = 60°. w = (6 × sin 60°, 6 × cos 60°) = (5.196, 3.0) m/s (east, north).
  2. Why this step: the drone moves over the ground with the sum of its airspeed arrow and the wind arrow, and this is its velocity when the motors stop. v = (0 + 5.196, 12 + 3.0) = (5.196, 15.0) m/s. Its size is |v| = √(5.196² + 15.0²) = √(27.0 + 225.0) = √252 = 15.87 m/s, and its direction is arctan(5.196 / 15.0) = 19.1° east of north.
  3. Why this step: with drag ignored, nothing pushes the drone sideways after the failure, so the horizontal velocity stays the same, and only gravity acts on the vertical motion. The mass of the parcel does not enter (2.2.9). The fall time is t = √(2·h / g) = √(2 × 40 / 9.8062) = √8.1581 = 2.856 s.
  4. Why this step: the drift is the horizontal velocity times the fall time, for each direction separately. North: 15.0 × 2.856 = 42.84 m. East: 5.196 × 2.856 = 14.84 m. Together: √(42.84² + 14.84²) = √(1835.3 + 220.2) = √2055.5 = 45.34 m.
  5. Why this step: the impact speed has a vertical part from the fall and a horizontal part that did not change. The vertical part is g·t = 9.8062 × 2.856 = 28.01 m/s downward. The total is √(252 + 28.01²) = √(252 + 784.56) = √1036.56 = 32.20 m/s.

Check your answer. A second method for the drift: the ground speed times the fall time, √252 × 2.856 = 45.34 m, equals the result of step 4. A comparison with Try it 30: a fall from 45 m ends at 29.70 m/s, and a fall from 40 m must end a little slower. The result, 28.01 m/s, is slower.

What if … drag were not ignored?

Show the answerThe fall would take longer and the vertical impact speed would be smaller, because drag grows with the speed and pushes against the fall. Drag would also pull the drone toward the speed of the air. The horizontal velocity would shrink from (5.196, 15.0) toward the wind velocity (5.196, 3.0), so the drone would land closer to the point below the failure than 45.34 m, but still downwind of it.
THINK 16 · THE MOTORS STOP IN A CLIMB, WITH A TAILWIND

Situation. A drone climbs at 30° above the horizontal on a north heading, with an airspeed of 10 m/s. The wind comes from the south (180°) at 5.0 m/s, so it is a tailwind. At the height of 25 m all motors stop. Drag is ignored (home field). Ideas used: the wind and the ground velocity of 2.1.12, the three-dimensional motion of 2.2.7 and the fall that starts with an upward velocity (2.2.9, Try it 33).

Questions. (a) What are the horizontal and the vertical parts of the ground velocity at the failure? (b) How long until the drone is on the ground? (c) How far north does it land, and how much of that is the effect of the wind? (d) How high does it rise first? (e) How fast is it at impact?

Think first.

  1. Which part of the velocity does the wind change, the horizontal part or the vertical part?
  2. The drone is still rising when the motors stop. What does gravity do to the rise?
  3. Which equation gives the time when you know the start height, the start velocity and the end height?
HintWrite the height as z = z₀ + vz·t − ½·g·t² and set z = 0. This is a quadratic equation in t. Keep the positive root.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: first split the airspeed into its horizontal and vertical parts. 10 × cos 30° = 8.660 m/s horizontally and 10 × sin 30° = 5.0 m/s upward.
  2. Why this step: the wind blows north, in the flight direction, and it has no vertical part. It adds to the horizontal part only. 8.660 + 5.0 = 13.660 m/s north. The vertical part stays 5.0 m/s.
  3. Why this step: after the failure the vertical motion is a straight-line problem with a = −g, z₀ = 25 and a start velocity of +5.0 m/s. Setting z = 0 gives t = (vz + √(vz² + 2·g·z₀)) / g. First the root: √(5.0² + 2 × 9.8062 × 25) = √515.31 = 22.70. Then t = (5.0 + 22.70) / 9.8062 = 2.825 s.
  4. Why this step: the horizontal distance is the horizontal ground speed times the time. 13.660 × 2.825 = 38.59 m. Without wind it would be 8.660 × 2.825 = 24.46 m, so the wind adds 38.59 − 24.46 = 14.13 m.
  5. Why this step: the highest point is where the upward velocity is used up. It is higher than 25 m by vz² / (2·g) = 5.0² / (2 × 9.8062) = 25.0 / 19.6124 = 1.27 m, so the height is 25 + 1.27 = 26.27 m, reached after 5.0 / 9.8062 = 0.510 s.
  6. Why this step: the impact speed has the final vertical velocity (equation 3 gives its size, 22.70 m/s downward) and the unchanged horizontal part. √(13.660² + 22.70²) = √(186.60 + 515.29) = √701.89 = 26.49 m/s.

Check your answer. A second method for the final vertical velocity: 5.0 − 9.8062 × 2.825 = 5.0 − 27.70 = −22.70 m/s, the same as the root in step 3 with a minus sign. The tailwind did not change the fall time. It only moved the landing point, by the wind speed times the fall time, 5.0 × 2.825, which is the 14.13 m of step 4 (to within the rounding).

What if … the wind were a headwind of 5.0 m/s (from the north)?

Show the answerThe fall time stays 2.825 s. The horizontal ground speed becomes 8.660 − 5.0 = 3.660 m/s, so the drone lands only 3.660 × 2.825 = 10.34 m ahead of the failure point.

2.2.21 Wind on a lap, and finding the wind

THINK 17 · A SQUARE LAP IN A STEADY WIND

Situation. A drone flies a square lap with four legs of 300 m each, in this order: north, east, south, west. It holds an airspeed of 15 m/s on every leg, and it points its nose so that it flies exactly along each leg. The wind comes from 300° at 6 m/s and is steady. Acceleration at the corners is ignored. Ideas used: ground = air + wind (2.1.12, the crosswind case) and the lap as a list of phases (2.2.8).

Questions. (a) What are the ground speed and the time of each leg? (b) What is the lap time, and how does it compare with the lap in still air? (c) Why does a steady wind always make a lap with equal legs longer, even though two legs get a tailwind?

Think first.

  1. Which part of the wind helps or opposes the drone along a leg, and which part pushes it sideways?
  2. The drone must not drift. What does it have to do with its nose, and what does that cost in speed along the leg?
  3. Is "15 plus the tailwind" the ground speed of a leg with a crosswind? Why not?
HintFor each leg, split the wind into a part along the leg and a part across it. The nose turns into the crosswind part, and only the rest of the airspeed, √(V² − w⊥²), is left for moving along the leg.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: first make the wind arrow. It blows toward 300° − 180° = 120°. w = (6 × sin 120°, 6 × cos 120°) = (5.20, −3.00) m/s (east, north): it blows to the east and to the south.
  2. Why this step: for each leg, the part of the wind along the leg is a tailwind (+) or a headwind (−), and the part across the leg is a crosswind. Leg 1 (north): the north part of the wind is −3.00, so there is a 3.00 m/s headwind, and the east part, 5.20, is across. The other legs follow in the same way (table below).
  3. Why this step: the drone must cancel the crosswind with its nose, so only √(V² − w⊥²) of the airspeed is left along the leg. The ground speed is that part plus the wind along the leg. The table gives all four rows.
  4. Why this step: add the four times for the lap, and compare with still air. 27.10 + 15.08 + 17.57 + 31.58 = 91.33 s. In still air: 4 × 300 / 15 = 80.0 s. The wind costs 91.33 − 80.0 = 11.33 s, which is 11.33 / 80.0 = 14 %.

How to read this table. The table has four rows, one for each leg of the lap. Read a row from left to right: the leg and its track, the part of the wind along the track, the part of the wind across the track, the ground speed, the time, and the direction of the nose. It matters for flying because the lap time is the sum of the four leg times. Here is every symbol. V = 15 m/s is the airspeed. w∥ is the wind along the track, in m/s: plus for a tailwind and minus for a headwind. w⊥ is the size of the wind across the track, in m/s; it pushes the drone sideways. √(V² − w⊥²) is the part of the airspeed that is left for moving along the track after the nose has turned into the crosswind; it is smaller than V. The ground speed vg is that part plus w∥, in m/s. The time is 300 / vg, in s. The nose direction is a compass bearing in degrees: the track bearing, turned by the angle arcsin(w⊥ / V) into the crosswind. Figure 4 draws the four rows on the lap.

The turn angles: arcsin(5.20 / 15) = 20.3° for the legs 1 and 3, and arcsin(3.00 / 15) = 11.5° for the legs 2 and 4. Whether the nose turns left or right depends on the side from which the crosswind comes.

Why the wind always costs time. Take two opposite legs, for example 1 and 3. They have the same crosswind and the same leftover airspeed c = √(V² − w⊥²), but their along-wind parts are +w and −w. Their times add up to L / (c − w) + L / (c + w) = 2·L·c / (c² − w²), and this is at least 2·L / c, because the denominator is smaller than c². Also c is at most V. So the sum is at least 2·L / V, the time in still air. With numbers: 27.10 + 17.57 = 44.67 s and 15.08 + 31.58 = 46.66 s, both more than the still-air 2 × 300 / 15 = 40.0 s. In words: the drone spends more time in the slow headwind leg than in the fast tailwind leg, and every crosswind wastes a part of the airspeed on the turn of the nose.

Check your answer. The ground speeds must lie between 15 − 6 = 9 and 15 + 6 = 21 m/s, the extremes of a wind of 6 m/s. They do: 9.50 to 19.90. A second method for leg 1: the nose arrow has 14.07 m/s along the track and 5.20 m/s across, against the crosswind. The wind has −3.00 along and 5.20 across. The sum is 14.07 − 3.00 = 11.07 m/s along the track and 0 across, so the drone does not drift.

What if … the wind were as fast as the airspeed, 15 m/s from 300°?

Show the answerThe wind arrow is then (15 × sin 120°, 15 × cos 120°) = (12.99, −7.50). On the west leg the headwind is 12.99 m/s and the crosswind is 7.50 m/s, so √(15² − 7.50²) = √168.75 = 12.99 is left, and the ground speed is 12.99 − 12.99 = 0.00 m/s. The drone cannot fly the west leg at all: the lap cannot be finished.
THINK 18 · FIND THE WIND FROM TWO GROUND SPEEDS

Situation. A drone holds an airspeed of 15 m/s on three legs, and the wind is steady. The log kept only the ground speeds (the size of the GPS velocity), not their directions. On leg A the nose pointed north and the ground speed was 20.22 m/s. On leg B the nose pointed east and the ground speed was 22.29 m/s. On leg C the nose pointed south and the ground speed was 13.00 m/s. Ideas used: ground = air + wind (2.1.12) and the length of a vector (2.2.7).

Questions. (a) What equation does each of the legs A and B give for the wind? (b) Find all winds that fit A and B. (c) Use leg C to choose one. (d) What are the wind speed and the bearing the wind comes from?

Think first.

  1. The log gives a length, not an arrow. Which winds give one length? What shape do they form?
  2. Two such shapes meet in how many points? What does this mean for the answer?
  3. What does a third measurement do?
HintFor leg A the ground arrow is (wE, 15 + wN), and its length is 20.22. Write the same for leg B, subtract the two equations, and the squared terms cancel.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: the ground arrow is the airspeed arrow plus the wind arrow, and the log gives its length. Leg A (nose north, airspeed arrow (0, 15)): wE² + (15 + wN)² = 20.22² = 408.85. Leg B (nose east, airspeed arrow (15, 0)): (15 + wE)² + wN² = 22.29² = 496.84.
  2. Why this step: subtract the second equation from the first. The terms wE², wN² and 225 appear in both and cancel, and only a linear equation is left: 30 × wN − 30 × wE = 408.85 − 496.84 = −87.99, so wN − wE = −87.99 / 30 = −2.933.
  3. Why this step: put wN = wE − 2.933 into the first equation, so that only one unknown is left. wE² + (12.067 + wE)² = 2 × wE² + 24.134 × wE + 145.61, and this equals 408.85. Therefore 2 × wE² + 24.134 × wE − 263.24 = 0.
  4. Why this step: a quadratic equation has two roots, and both are possible winds. wE = (−24.134 ± √(24.134² + 8 × 263.24)) / 4 = (−24.134 ± √2688.37) / 4 = (−24.134 ± 51.849) / 4. The roots are wE = 6.929 and wE = −18.996.
  5. Why this step: the north part follows from the linear equation. wN = 6.929 − 2.933 = 3.996 and wN = −18.996 − 2.933 = −21.929.
  6. Why this step: turn each wind into a speed and a bearing. Wind 1: √(6.929² + 3.996²) = √(48.01 + 15.97) = √63.98 = 8.00 m/s, toward arctan(6.929 / 3.996) = 60.0°, so it comes from 240°. Wind 2: √(18.996² + 21.929²) = √(360.85 + 480.88) = √841.73 = 29.01 m/s, toward 180° + arctan(18.996 / 21.929) = 180° + 40.9°, so it comes from 40.9°.
  7. Why this step: leg C decides. For the nose south the airspeed arrow is (0, −15). Wind 1 predicts √(6.929² + (3.996 − 15)²) = √(48.01 + 121.09) = √169.10 = 13.00 m/s, which is the measured value. Wind 2 predicts √(18.996² + (−21.929 − 15)²) = √(360.85 + 1363.75) = √1724.60 = 41.53 m/s, which is not. So the wind is 8.0 m/s from 240°.

Check your answer. Put wind 1 back into the equations of legs A and B. Leg A: √(6.929² + (15 + 3.996)²) = √(48.01 + 360.85) = √408.86 = 20.22 m/s. Leg B: √((15 + 6.929)² + 3.996²) = √(480.88 + 15.97) = √496.85 = 22.29 m/s. Both agree with the log. Wind 2 would be stronger than the airspeed (29 m/s against 15 m/s), which is also a reason to doubt it.

What if … the drone now flies a leg with the nose pointing west? Predict its ground speed.

Show the answerThe airspeed arrow is (−15, 0), so the ground speed is √((6.929 − 15)² + 3.996²) = √(65.14 + 15.97) = √81.11 = 9.01 m/s. A found wind lets you predict any leg.

2.2.22 Takeoff and landing rolls at Denver, in a wind, with a load

THINK 19 · WHICH RUNWAY DIRECTION, AND IS 150 m ENOUGH?

Situation. A fixed-wing aircraft works at Denver (ρ = 1.049 kg/m³, g = 9.7965 m/s²) from a strip of 150 m, which can be used as runway 27 (heading 270°, toward the west) or as runway 09 (heading 90°, toward the east). The wind comes from 240° at 8.0 m/s. In 2.2.10 the same aircraft, without a load, takes off at Denver with 27.0 m/s and 2.141 m/s² (row 4 of the table there). A load now makes it 5 % heavier (mass factor 1.05). The takeoff speed grows with the square root of the mass, and the acceleration falls in proportion to 1 / mass (Think 8). At touchdown its airspeed is 25.0 m/s and its brakes give −2.0 m/s² (both made up). The roll ends at the ground speed that matches the airspeed at liftoff: ground speed = airspeed − headwind component. Ideas used: the rolls and the Denver numbers of 2.2.10, and the wind of 2.1.12.

Questions. (a) What are the headwind and the crosswind components for each runway direction? (b) What are the takeoff rolls and the landing rolls? (c) Which direction should be used, and does 150 m suffice? (d) What headwind makes the takeoff just fit?

Think first.

  1. The angle that counts is between the wind direction (where it comes from) and the runway heading. Which angle is it for each runway?
  2. A headwind does not change the acceleration in this simple model. What does it change?
  3. Would the aircraft fit on the strip in still air? Guess first, and check at the end.
HintThe headwind component is w·cos(angle) and the crosswind is w·sin(angle), with the angle between "wind from" and the heading. Then use s = (ground speed)² / (2·a).
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: first get the loaded aircraft. √1.05 = 1.0247, so V = 27.0 × 1.0247 = 27.67 m/s, and a = 2.141 / 1.05 = 2.039 m/s². Then 2·a = 2 × 2.039 = 4.078.
  2. Why this step: split the wind for each heading. Runway 27: the wind comes from 240°, the heading is 270°, so the angle is 30°. Headwind 8.0 × cos 30° = 6.93 m/s, crosswind 8.0 × sin 30° = 4.00 m/s. Runway 09: the angle is 240° − 90° = 150°. 8.0 × cos 150° = −6.93 m/s, which means a tailwind of 6.93 m/s, and 8.0 × sin 150° = 4.00 m/s across.
  3. Why this step: the aircraft lifts off at the airspeed V, and the wind supplies part of it, so the ground speed at liftoff is V minus the headwind. The roll then follows from equation 3 with the ground speed, as in 2.2.10. The table gives the rolls for the five cases (takeoff on 27, takeoff on 09, takeoff in still air, landing on 27, landing on 09).

How to read this table. The table has five rows, one for each case. Read a row from left to right: the case, the part of the wind along the runway, the ground speed, the roll length, and whether the roll fits on the strip of 150 m. It matters for flying because the roll length decides whether a runway direction can be used. Here is every symbol. The headwind is positive and the tailwind negative, in m/s. For a takeoff the ground speed is the takeoff airspeed 27.67 m/s minus the headwind; for a landing it is the touchdown airspeed 25.0 m/s minus the headwind, in m/s. The roll is s = (ground speed)² / (2·a) in m, with 2·a = 4.078 m/s² for the takeoff and 2·a = 4.0 m/s² for the landing. Figure 6 draws the five rows as bars.

  2. Why this step: for (d), work backward. The roll may be at most 150 m, so the ground speed may be at most √(2·a·150) = √(4.078 × 150) = √611.70 = 24.73 m/s. Check your answer. The guess of the second question was probably "yes, it fits in still air". The table shows the opposite: in still air the loaded aircraft needs 187.75 m, so without the wind it cannot use this strip at all. Even the aircraft without the load needs 170.2 m (row 4 of 2.2.10). The wind of 6.93 m/s along the runway is what makes the strip usable, and the tailwind runway is the worst case, with 293.57 m, nearly twice the strip.

What if … the headwind component were only 3.00 m/s?

Show the answerThe ground speed would be 27.67 − 3.00 = 24.67 m/s, and the roll 24.67² / 4.078 = 608.61 / 4.078 = 149.24 m. The takeoff would just fit (the limit is 2.94 m/s). Any wind weaker than 3.39 m/s from 240° would make the takeoff impossible on this strip.

2.2.23 Stopping and climbing with a payload, in a tailwind and in an updraft

THINK 20 · STOP OVER THE PAD, WITH A PARCEL AND A TAILWIND

Situation. The loaded quadrotor of Problem 5 on page 2.1b (1.5 kg, with a parcel of 2.0 kg, four motors of at most 15 N each, so Tmax = 60 N) flies north toward a landing pad, 20.0 m ahead. Its airspeed is 12 m/s and a tailwind of 6 m/s (wind from 180°) blows north. It also descends at 3.0 m/s. It must arrive at the pad with both the horizontal and the vertical speed equal to 0, with constant accelerations (home field, drag ignored). The default horizontal acceleration limit of the planner of PX4 is 3.0 m/s² (row 3 of 2.2.15) [29]. Ideas used: braking (2.2.6), the tilt limit and the payload (2.1.10, Problem 5) and the wind (2.1.12). Newton's second law, F = m·a, is taken as given, as on page 2.2b (section 2.3 explains it).

Questions. (a) What constant horizontal deceleration does the stop need? (b) What upward acceleration stops the descent in the same time, and how far below the drone is the pad? (c) What thrust is needed, and how much of the maximum is that? (d) Could the drone stop in 20.0 m with the default acceleration limit of the planner? What airspeed would it need then?

Think first.

  1. The horizontal speed is the ground speed. Is that the airspeed?
  2. Both speeds must reach 0 together. What do they share?
  3. The thrust must hold the weight and also make the two accelerations. How do you combine the three parts into one number?
HintThe thrust arrow has an upward part m·(g + aup) and a horizontal part m·ah. They are the two short sides of a right triangle, and T is the long side.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: the distance to the pad is a ground distance, so the speed that counts is the ground speed: airspeed plus tailwind. v = 12 + 6 = 18 m/s. The weight of the loaded drone is W = 3.5 × 9.8062 = 34.322 N.
  2. Why this step: equation 3 with v = 0 gives the braking acceleration. ah = v² / (2·d) = 18² / (2 × 20.0) = 324 / 40.0 = 8.10 m/s². The time of the stop is the distance divided by the average speed. t = 2·d / v = 2 × 20.0 / 18 = 2.222 s.
  3. Why this step: the vertical speed must also reach 0 after the same time, so the upward acceleration follows from equation 1. aup = 3.0 / 2.222 = 1.35 m/s². The pad is below the drone by ½ × 3.0 × 2.222 = 3.33 m.
  4. Why this step: the rotors must make the force that holds the weight (g), makes the upward acceleration, and makes the horizontal braking. The upward part is m·(g + aup) and the horizontal part is m·ah. T = m·√(ah² + (g + aup)²) = 3.5 × √(8.10² + (9.8062 + 1.35)²) = 3.5 × √(65.61 + 124.46) = 3.5 × √190.07 = 48.25 N.
  5. Why this step: compare with the limit of one rotor. 48.25 / 4 = 12.06 N per rotor, which is 12.06 / 15 = 80.4 % of the maximum. The hover needs 34.322 / 4 / 15 = 57.2 %. The stop is possible by 20 % of the thrust.
  6. Why this step: for (d), test the default limit of the planner. With 3.0 m/s² the stop from 18 m/s needs 18² / (2 × 3.0) = 324 / 6.0 = 54.0 m, far more than 20.0 m. The largest ground speed from which the planner can stop in 20.0 m is √(2 × 3.0 × 20.0) = √120.0 = 10.95 m/s, which is an airspeed of only 10.95 − 6.0 = 4.95 m/s in this tailwind.

Check your answer. The thrust of the stop is larger than the thrust of the hover, as it must be: 48.25 / 34.322 = 1.406, that is 41 % more. A stop without the tailwind (ground speed 12 m/s) would need 12² / (2 × 3.0) = 144 / 6.0 = 24.0 m even at the default limit. So the tailwind turns an easy stop into one that needs a much harder brake: the stop distance grows with the square of the ground speed.

What if … the tailwind were 12 m/s (ground speed 24 m/s)?

Show the answerThen ah = 24² / (2 × 20.0) = 14.40 m/s², t = 2 × 20.0 / 24 = 1.667 s and aup = 3.0 / 1.667 = 1.80 m/s². The thrust is T = 3.5 × √(14.40² + (9.8062 + 1.80)²) = 3.5 × √(207.36 + 134.70) = 3.5 × √342.06 = 64.73 N, which is more than the 60 N that the motors can make. The stop is impossible: the drone would arrive too fast, or it must slow down earlier.
THINK 21 · A CLIMB TO A GATE IN A HEADWIND AND AN UPDRAFT

Situation. GPS is lost, so the drone can only hold its velocity relative to the air. It starts at a height of 2 m. A gate is 60 m ahead (east) at a height of 20 m. The pilot sets an air velocity of 10 m/s east and 3.0 m/s up. The wind has a headwind of 4.0 m/s (from the east) and, near a ridge, an updraft of 1.5 m/s. The motors can give a climb rate of at most 3.0 m/s relative to the air (made up). Ideas used: the motion in three dimensions (2.2.7) and the wind that also blows up or down (2.1.12, Try it 31).

Questions. (a) What is the ground velocity with the pilot's setting, and at what height does the drone pass x = 60 m? (b) What air climb rate would hit the gate? (c) A downdraft of 1.5 m/s (behind the ridge) replaces the updraft. Can the drone still reach the gate at the same horizontal ground speed? If not, what is the best it can do?

Think first.

  1. Does the wind change the horizontal part, the vertical part, or both?
  2. Which speed fixes the time to reach x = 60 m: the airspeed or the ground speed?
  3. For (c), which limit is used up, and what can the pilot change?
HintGround velocity = air velocity + wind velocity, part by part. Find the time from the horizontal part, then the height from the vertical part.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: add the air velocity and the wind, one part at a time. The headwind is −4.0 east, the updraft is +1.5 up. (10 − 4.0, 3.0 + 1.5) = (6.0, 4.5) m/s (east, up).
  2. Why this step: the horizontal ground speed fixes the time. t = 60 / 6.0 = 10.0 s. The height at that time is 2 + 4.5 × 10.0 = 47.0 m, which is 27 m above the gate.
  3. Why this step: work backward. The drone must rise 18 m in the same 10.0 s, so the ground climb rate must be 18 / 10.0 = 1.8 m/s. The updraft supplies 1.5 m/s of it, so the air climb rate is 1.8 − 1.5 = 0.3 m/s.

Check your answer. With 0.3 m/s the ground climb rate is 0.3 + 1.5 = 1.8 m/s, and in 10.0 s the drone rises 1.8 × 10.0 = 18.0 m, from 2 m to 20 m. The air climb rate 0.3 m/s is below the limit of 3.0 m/s.

What if … a downdraft of 1.5 m/s replaces the updraft (part c)?

Show the answerFor the same 10.0 s, the air climb rate would have to be 1.8 − (−1.5) = 3.3 m/s, above the limit of 3.0 m/s. The best ground climb rate is 3.0 + (−1.5) = 1.5 m/s. The climb of 18 m then needs at least 18 / 1.5 = 12.0 s, so the horizontal ground speed may be at most 60 / 12.0 = 5.0 m/s. Against the 4.0 m/s headwind, the pilot must set an airspeed of 5.0 + 4.0 = 9.0 m/s. The drone must fly more slowly to reach the gate at the right height.

2.2.24 Gusts and sensor errors

THINK 22 · A GUST ON THE WAY TO THE GATE

Situation. A drone holds an airspeed of 12 m/s toward a gate 120 m ahead, straight into the wind, and it has no way to correct for the wind. The planner predicted the arrival with the steady wind of 4.0 m/s. A gust arrives. The headwind follows this profile (made up). It is 4.0 m/s until t = 6 s. It then grows in a straight line to 10.0 m/s at t = 8 s. It stays at 10.0 m/s until t = 12 s. It falls in a straight line back to 4.0 m/s at t = 14 s, and then stays there. The profile is a simplified version of the wind model of Gazebo [31]: in the reference rows GZ-46 and GZ-47 (WindEffects.cc @ 9e3b78f) the wind magnitude follows a commanded value with a first-order lag, whose default characteristic time is 1.0 s, and a sine can be added. Here the rise is a straight line, so that every distance is the area of a trapezoid. Ideas used: the lap as phases (2.2.8), the area under a speed graph (2.2.4) and ground = air + wind (2.1.12).

Questions. (a) Integrate the position phase by phase. (b) When does the drone reach the gate? How much later than planned? (c) Where is the drone at the planned time of arrival?

Think first.

  1. The ground speed is 12 m/s minus the headwind. How does it change in each phase?
  2. In a phase where the speed changes in a straight line, which average speed gives the distance?
  3. The last phase has no fixed end. What is the unknown there?
HintThe distance of a phase is the area under the speed graph: a rectangle for a steady speed, a trapezoid for a straight change. Add the areas until you have 120 m.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: the ground speed is the airspeed minus the headwind, so it is 12.0 − 4.0 = 8.0 m/s at the start and 12.0 − 10.0 = 2.0 m/s in the gust. The table gives the phases one by one.

How to read this table. The table has five rows, one for each phase. Read a row from left to right: the time span, the headwind, the ground speed, the distance of the phase, and the position at its end. It matters for flying because the position at the planned time shows how far the gust moved the arrival. Here is every symbol. The headwind is in m/s; an arrow means a straight change. The ground speed is vg = 12.0 − headwind, in m/s. The distance of a phase is the area under the speed graph, in m: speed times time for a steady speed, and ½ × (first speed + last speed) × time for a straight change. The position at the end is the sum of the distances so far, in m. Phase 5 has no end time yet, so its time is computed from the distance that is left. THINK 23 · HOW LONG UNTIL A BIAS MAKES THE DRONE MISS A GATE?

Situation. A drone flies straight at 10 m/s without GPS. Its estimator [40] estimates the position, the velocity, the attitude and the accelerometer biases (row 10 of the autopilot map), and it keeps the position by adding up the accelerometer data. The estimate of one accelerometer bias is wrong by b = 0.04 m/s² (made up), so the error acts like a constant false acceleration. Gates stand every 40 m. A gate is missed when the position error is more than 1.5 m (made up). Ideas used: position from a constant acceleration (2.2.5, equation 2), the tilt of 2.1.10 and the estimator row of page 2.2a.

Questions. (a) After how many seconds, and how many meters, does the error reach 1.5 m? (b) What are the errors at the first three gates, and which gate is missed first? (c) Which tilt error of the attitude makes the same false acceleration? (d) How does the answer change if the bias is four times smaller?

Think first.

  1. A constant false acceleration b, starting at rest, gives a position error of what size after a time t?
  2. The gates are 40 m apart. At what times does the drone pass them?
  3. Does halving the bias double the time to the limit?
HintEquation 2 with v₀ = 0: the error is ½·b·t². For the tilt: a drone tilted by δ measures the part g·sin δ of gravity as a horizontal acceleration.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: the estimator integrates the false acceleration twice, so the error is the position of a constant acceleration, ½·b·t². Setting it equal to the limit E gives t = √(2·E / b) = √(2 × 1.5 / 0.04) = √75.0 = 8.66 s. The distance flown is 10 × 8.66 = 86.6 m.
  2. Why this step: the gates are passed at 40 / 10 = 4.0 s, 8.0 s and 12.0 s. The errors there are ½ × 0.04 × 4.0² = 0.32 m, ½ × 0.04 × 8.0² = 1.28 m and ½ × 0.04 × 12.0² = 2.88 m. The first two are below 1.5 m; the third gate is missed. The velocity error there is 0.04 × 12.0 = 0.48 m/s.
  3. Why this step: a tilt error δ of the estimated attitude lets the estimator mistake part of gravity for a horizontal acceleration, b = g·sin δ. Solve for δ: δ = asin(b / g) = asin(0.04 / 9.8062) = asin(0.004079) = 0.234°. A tilt error of a quarter of a degree is enough.
  4. Why this step: for a four times smaller bias, b = 0.01 m/s², use the same formula. t = √(2 × 1.5 / 0.01) = √300.0 = 17.32 s.

Check your answer. The time to the limit scales with 1 / √b. A bias four times smaller gives a time 17.32 / 8.66 = 2.0 times longer, not four times longer. The errors grow with t², so doubling the time gives four times the error: 0.32 m at 4.0 s and 1.28 m at 8.0 s.

What if … the bias is 0.01 m/s² (part d): how many gates can the drone pass?

Show the answerThe errors at the gates at 16.0 s and 20.0 s are ½ × 0.01 × 16.0² = 1.28 m and ½ × 0.01 × 20.0² = 2.00 m. The drone passes four gates (at 4, 8, 12 and 16 s) and misses the fifth, after 200 m.

2.2.25 Planning: an S-curve with a parcel, and the best plan

THINK 24 · THE S-CURVE WITH A PARCEL: IS THE CRUISE SPEED REACHED?

Situation. A delivery drone must move 20.0 m from rest at one pad to rest at the next pad. The wanted cruise speed is 6.0 m/s. The jerk limit has the default of PX4, J = 4.0 m/s³ (row 4 of 2.2.15) [29, 47]. To protect a parcel, the operator lowers the acceleration limit from the default A = 3.0 m/s² to A = 2.0 m/s², the smallest value that row 3 allows. The plan is a speed-up S-curve, a cruise, and a slow-down S-curve of the same size. Ideas used: the S-curve of 2.2.14 and the cost of the jerk limit of 2.2.16 (the same method as Think 12).

Questions. (a) With the default A, how long does the run take? (b) With A = 2.0 m/s², does the drone reach 6.0 m/s? (c) If not, what peak speed does it reach, and how much time does the lower limit cost?

Think first.

  1. How much track do the two S-curves to 6.0 m/s need together, for each A?
  2. If the track is too short, which number is unknown?
  3. Does the distance rule of 2.2.17 make the real planner more or less careful than this plan?
HintOne S-curve to V covers V·T / 2 with T = V / A + A / J. If two do not fit, each half covers half of the run, and V is the unknown, as in Think 12.
Show the solution
  1. Why this step: first the default, as the reference. One speed-up: T = 6.0 / 3.0 + 3.0 / 4.0 = 2.75 s and x = 6.0 × 2.75 / 2 = 8.25 m. Two together: 2 × 8.25 = 16.5 m, so a cruise of 20.0 − 16.5 = 3.5 m is left, which takes 3.5 / 6.0 = 0.583 s. The run takes 2 × 2.75 + 0.583 = 6.083 s.
  2. Why this step: now the lowered limit. The test of 2.2.14 first: A² / J = 2.0² / 4.0 = 1.0, which is below 6.0, so A is reached. T = V / A + A / J = 6.0 / 2.0 + 2.0 / 4.0 = 3.0 + 0.5 = 3.5 s, and one speed-up covers V·T / 2 = 6.0 × 3.5 / 2 = 10.5 m. Two of them need 2 × 10.5 = 21.0 m, which is more than 20.0 m. So the drone does not reach 6.0 m/s.
  3. Why this step: V is now the unknown. Each half covers 10.0 m, and the equation of Think 12 reads V² + (A² / J) × V − 2 × A × 10.0 = V² + 1.0 × V − 40.0 = 0. V = ½ × (−1.0 + √(1.0² + 4 × 40.0)) = ½ × (−1.0 + √161.0) = ½ × (−1.0 + 12.689) = 5.84 m/s.
  4. Why this step: the time follows from the same formula for one half, doubled. Thalf = 5.84 / 2.0 + 0.5 = 2.92 + 0.5 = 3.42 s, and 2 × 3.42 = 6.84 s. The lower limit costs 6.84 − 6.083 = 0.76 s.

Check your answer. One half must cover 10.0 m: 5.84 × 3.42 / 2 = 9.99 m. The peak 5.84 m/s is below the wanted 6.0 m/s, and the time is longer than the default's, as it must be. The real planner of PX4 also cuts the speed with the distance to the target (2.2.17). With A = 2.0 and J = 4.0 we get b = 4 × 2.0² / 4.0 = 4.0, and in the middle of the run, 10.0 m from the target, the rule allows only ½ × (−4.0 + √(4.0² + 8 × 2.0 × 10.0)) = ½ × (−4.0 + √176.0) = ½ × (−4.0 + 13.266) = 4.63 m/s. So the real planner is more careful than this plan: its speed in the middle of the run is at most 4.63 m/s, below 5.84 m/s.

What if … the run were 21.0 m long?

Show the answerThen each half covers 10.5 m, and the drone with A = 2.0 m/s² just reaches 6.0 m/s: 2 × 10.5 = 21.0 m, with no cruise at all.
THINK 25 · THE FASTEST PLAN, AND WHY CRUISING EARLIER IS SLOWER

Situation. A drone must fly L = 200 m in a straight line from rest to rest. Its speed limit is vmax = 16 m/s, and it can speed up and brake with the acceleration a = 4.0 m/s² (both made up). It accelerates, may cruise, and brakes. Which plan gives the shortest time? A student proposes to reach a lower cruise speed "to be safe" and to cruise earlier. Ideas used: the straight run (2.2.6) and the S-curve cost (2.2.16).

Questions. (a) Find the time of each plan with the cruise speeds 16, 14, 12 and 10 m/s. (b) Which plan is the fastest, and why? (c) By how much does the time grow if the planner has a jerk limit J = 4.0 m/s³ (S-curve)?

Think first.

  1. For one plan, the speed-up and the braking use the same time and distance. What is left for the cruise?
  2. A higher cruise speed costs more time in the speed-up and saves time in the cruise. Which effect is bigger?
  3. Should the drone accelerate with less than its largest acceleration?
HintFor a plan with the cruise speed v: speed-up time v / a, speed-up distance v² / (2·a), cruise distance L − 2 × v² / (2·a), then the cruise time is that distance divided by v.
Show the solution

Read a row from left to right: the cruise speed, the time of one speed-up, the distance of one speed-up, the cruise distance, the cruise time and the total time. The braking mirrors the speed-up. It matters for flying because it shows how the total time depends on the cruise speed. ✓ Check yourself

  1. A weather report says "wind from 090° at 5 m/s". Toward which bearing does the air move, and what are the east and north parts of the wind arrow?
    answerThe air moves toward 270°, west. The wind arrow is (5 × sin 270°, 5 × cos 270°) = (−5, 0) m/s.
  2. A drone flies a leg of 90 m and back at the airspeed of 15 m/s in a wind of 5 m/s along the leg. Does the wind change the round-trip time?
    answerYes: 90 / (15 − 5) + 90 / (15 + 5) = 9.0 + 4.5 = 13.5 s, against 2 × 90 / 15 = 12.0 s in still air. The slow leg lasts longer than the fast leg.
  3. Why does a headwind shorten a takeoff roll, when the acceleration does not change?
    answerThe roll ends at the airspeed for liftoff. The wind supplies part of it, so the ground speed at liftoff is the airspeed minus the headwind, and the roll is (ground speed)² / (2·a).
  4. A false acceleration b acts for a time t. How large is the position error, and what happens when t doubles?
    answerThe error is ½·b·t². It grows four times when the time doubles.
  5. When does a drone with the speed limit vmax and the acceleration a, from rest to rest over L, cruise at all?
    answerIt cruises when the track holds both the speed-up and the braking: L ≥ vmax² / a. For vmax = 16 m/s and a = 4.0 m/s² that is 16² / 4.0 = 64.0 m.

★ Summary

  • Wind. A wind is named after the bearing it comes from. The air arrow points to that bearing plus 180°. Ground velocity = airspeed arrow + wind arrow (Think 15, 17, 18, 21).
  • Crosswind. On a leg with a crosswind w⊥ the nose turns into the wind and only √(V² − w⊥²) is left for the way along the leg (Think 17).
  • Falling. After a motor failure the horizontal ground velocity stays (without drag), and the vertical motion is a free fall with a start velocity: t = (vz + √(vz² + 2·g·z₀)) / g (Think 15, 16).
  • Runways. The roll uses the ground speed at liftoff: airspeed minus headwind. The roll grows with the square of the mass (Think 8, 19).
  • Payload. The thrust must hold the weight and make the acceleration: T = m·√(ah² + (g + aup)²) (Think 20).
  • Gusts. Integrate phase by phase: the distance of a phase is the area under the ground-speed graph (Think 22).
  • Sensor errors. A constant false acceleration b gives the error ½·b·t² (Think 10, 23).
  • Plans. The fastest rest-to-rest plan accelerates fully, cruises at the limit and brakes fully; T(v) = v / a + L / v. A lower A or a jerk limit costs time (Think 24, 25).
